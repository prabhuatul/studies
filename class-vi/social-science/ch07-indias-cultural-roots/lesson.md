## Overview

This chapter traces the roots of Indian culture — pictured as an old tree with many roots feeding one trunk, from which many branches grow. It covers the **Vedas and Vedic culture** (the oldest root), the new **schools of thought** that emerged in the 1st millennium BCE — **Buddhism**, **Jainism**, and briefly the materialist **Chārvāka** school — and finally **folk and tribal traditions**, showing how all these strands influenced and enriched each other over millennia to shape "India, that is Bharat."

## Core Concepts

### 1. India's cultural roots — the tree metaphor

Indian culture is several millennia old and, like an ancient tree, has many roots feeding one common trunk, from which many branches grow — art, literature, science, medicine, religion, the art of governance, martial arts, and **schools of thought** (groups of thinkers or spiritual seekers sharing similar ideas about human life and the world).

Some of India's cultural roots go back to the Indus/Harappan/Sindhu–Sarasvatī civilisation (Chapter 6). Over time hundreds of schools of thought emerged; this chapter looks at a few early ones that shaped India's unique personality.

### 2. The Vedas and Vedic Culture

**a. What are the Vedas?**

There are four Vedas — the **Ṛig Veda**, the **Yajur Veda**, the **Sāma Veda** and the **Atharva Veda** — India's, and among the world's, most ancient texts. They consist of thousands of hymns (prayers as poems and songs) that were **recited orally, not written**, composed in the Sapta Sindhava region (Chapter 5). It is hard to date the Ṛig Veda precisely; experts propose anywhere from the 5th to the 2nd millennium BCE — so for anything between 100 and 200 generations these texts have been committed to memory through rigorous training and passed on orally with hardly any alterations. This meticulous transmission is why, in 2008, **UNESCO recognised Vedic chanting as "a masterpiece of the oral and intangible heritage of humanity."**

The hymns were composed by **rishis** (male seers) and **rishikas** (female seers) in an early form of Sanskrit, addressed to many deities (Varuṇa, Mitra, Sarasvatī, Uṣhas, Indra, Agni, and more). Together with the seers, these deities sustained **ritam** — truth and order in human life and the cosmos. The early rishis and rishikas saw the many gods and goddesses as **one**, not separate beings, as this hymn says:

> *ekam sat viprā bahudhā vadanti* — "The Existent [the supreme reality] is one, but sages give it many names."

In this worldview, some values were especially important, beginning with 'Truth' — often another name for God. The closing mantras of the Ṛig Veda call for unity among people: *"Come together, speak together; common be your mind, may your thoughts agree … United be your purpose, united your heart … may your thoughts be united, so all may agree!"*

**b. Vedic society**

Early Vedic society was organised into different **janas**, or 'clans' — larger groups of people. The Ṛig Veda alone lists over 30 janas — for instance the Bharatas, the Purus, the Kurus, the Yadus, the Turvashas — each associated with a particular region of the northwest of the Subcontinent.

Not much is known of how the janas governed themselves. The Vedas give only a few clues, through words like **rājā** (king or ruler), and **sabha** and **samiti** (both referring to a collective gathering or assembly). Many professions are mentioned in the Vedic texts, such as agriculturist, weaver, potter, builder, carpenter, **healer**, dancer, barber, priest, etc.

Two dashed bands run alongside: **Upanishads** (spanning roughly 1000 BCE to past 1 CE) and **Vedic schools of thought** (spanning roughly 500 BCE to past 500 CE) — showing these developed after the earliest Vedic hymns and overlapped with the rise of Buddhism and Jainism.

**c. Vedic schools of thought**

Vedic culture developed many rituals (**yajña**, often read as 'yagya') directed towards deities for individual or collective benefit and wellbeing. Daily rituals were generally offerings to **Agni**, the deity of fire, but rituals grew more complex over time.

A group of texts called the **Upanishads** built on Vedic concepts and introduced new ones: **rebirth** (taking birth again and again) and **karma** (our actions, or their results). One school of thought, generally known as **Vedanta**, held that everything — human life, nature, the universe — is one divine essence called **brahman** (not the god Brahmā), or sometimes just *tat* ('that'). Two well-known mantras express this:

> *aham brahmāsmi* — "I am brahman" (I am divine)
> *tat tvam asi* — "You are That"

The Upanishads also introduced **ātman**, or Self — the divine essence in every being, ultimately one with brahman. It follows that everything in the world is connected and interdependent — explaining the common prayer *sarve bhavantu sukhinah*, "May all creatures be happy," which continues by wishing them free from disease and sorrow.

Early in the 1st millennium BCE several more schools grew out of the Vedas. One was **Yoga**, which developed methods to realise brahman in one's own consciousness. Together, these schools became the foundations of what we call 'Hinduism' today.

**Stories from the Upanishads** (many told to test whether questions on these ideas come from men, women or children):

- *Śhvetaketu and the seed of reality (Chhāndogya Upanishad)* — Rishi Uddālaka Āruṇi sent his son Śhvetaketu to a gurukula to learn the Vedas. When Śhvetaketu returned 12 years later, proud of his learning, his father tested him with questions on the nature of brahman that he could not answer. Uddālaka explained that brahman, though invisible, is everywhere — just as the seed of a banyan fruit seems empty but already contains the future tree, or as all kinds of pots are made from the same clay. Everything around us has emerged from the same essence — brahman. He concluded: *"Everything consists of this subtle essence... You are That, Śhvetaketu."*
- *Nachiketa and his quest (Katha Upanishad)* — A man was giving away all his possessions in a ritual. His son Nachiketa kept asking which god he would be offered to, until his angry father said, "I give you to Yama" — the god of death. Nachiketa went to Yama's world and, after a long wait, met the god and asked what happens after the death of the body. Yama tried to avoid answering, but the boy persisted. Pleased, Yama explained that the **ātman**, or self, is hidden within all creatures: it is neither born nor does it die; it is immortal. Nachiketa then returned to his father, who welcomed him joyfully.
- *The debate of Gārgī and Yājñavalkya (Brihadāranyaka Upanishad)* — King Janaka announced a prize for the winner of a philosophical debate. The rishi Yājñavalkya defeated many scholars until Gārgī, a rishikā, questioned him on the nature of the world and finally of brahman. Yājñavalkya asked her to stop; later she resumed, and he went on to explain how brahman is what makes the world, the seasons, the rivers and everything else possible.

### 3. Buddhism

About two-and-a-half millenniums ago, a young prince named **Siddhārtha Gautama** was born in **Lumbini** (today in Nepal). Scholars propose different years for his birth depending on their sources; the textbook uses **560 BCE** as an approximate year (it makes no difference to the story here — see Chapter 4 for more).

Siddhārtha grew up living a protected life in the palace. One day, at the age of 29, driven through the city in a chariot, he saw for the first time an old man, a sick man, a dead body, and also an **ascetic** who appeared happy and at peace. Following this experience, he decided to give up his palace life, leaving behind his wife and son. Travelling on foot as an ascetic, meeting other ascetics and scholars, he searched for the root cause of suffering in human life. After meditating for many days under a pipal tree at **Bodh Gaya** (today in Bihar), he attained enlightenment: he realised that **avidyā** (ignorance) and **attachment** are the source of human suffering, and conceived a method to remove these two causes.

Siddhārtha then became known as the **Buddha**, meaning the 'enlightened' or 'awakened' one. He began teaching what he had realised, including the idea of **ahimsa** — generally translated 'non-violence', but originally meaning 'non-hurting' or 'non-injuring'. He also insisted on sincere inner discipline, expressed simply in one of his sayings:

> *"Not by water is one made pure, though many people may bathe here [in sacred rivers]. But one is pure in whom truth and dharma reside. Conquering oneself is greater than conquering a thousand men on the battlefield a thousand times."*

The Buddha founded the **Sangha**, a community of **bhikshus** or monks (and, later, **bhikshunīs** or nuns) who dedicated themselves to practising and spreading his teachings. His influence on India, and indeed the whole of Asia, was enormous, as we will discover later; it is still perceptible today.

*Figure (p.114):* a stone panel, about 1,800 years old, showing the Buddha teaching, surrounded by a group of followers with hands raised and offerings; carved architectural pillars border the scene.

**The Jātaka tales** delighted generations of Indian children and adults with stories of the Buddha's former births, expressing Buddhist values simply. In a well-known tale, the Buddha was the king of a large troop of monkeys living near a huge tree bearing fruit of divine fragrance and taste. Despite the monkey-king's instructions that no fruit should escape, one ripe fruit fell into a stream and was carried to a human king's palace, whose taste so enchanted him that he ordered his soldiers to find the tree. They found it and attacked the monkeys, who could not cross the stream on their own. The much larger monkey-king caught hold of a tree on the other bank and let the other monkeys use his body as a bridge to cross — though he was severely bruised and eventually died from the effort. The human king, watching from a distance, was deeply moved by the monkey-king's selfless sacrifice and reflected on the role of a king with respect to his subjects.

*Figure (p.116–117):* a stone panel (at Bharhut, Madhya Pradesh) depicting the story of the monkey-king.

### 4. Jainism

Jainism is another important school of thought that became widespread at the same time as Buddhism, though its roots are said to be much more ancient. Just like Siddhārtha Gautama, **Prince Vardhamāna** was born into a royal family in the early 6th century BCE, near the city of **Vaishālī** in modern-day **Bihar**. At age 30 he left home in search of spiritual knowledge, practised an ascetic discipline, and after 12 years achieved 'infinite knowledge' or supreme wisdom. He became known as **Mahāvīra**, 'great hero', and began preaching what he had realised.

*Figure (p.115):* a traditional painting of Mahāvīra seated cross-legged in white robes, framed by an ornate arch with serpent and floral motifs.

The word **'Jain'** (*jaina*) comes from *jina*, meaning 'conqueror' — not conquest of territory or enemies, but conquest of ignorance and attachment, to reach enlightenment.

Jain teachings include **ahimsa**, **anekāntavāda** and **aparigraha** — ideas shared to a large extent with Buddhism and the Vedantic school:

- **Anekāntavāda** ('not just one' aspect) — truth has many aspects and cannot be fully described by any single statement.
- **Aparigraha** ('non-possession') — detachment from material possessions, limiting oneself to what is truly necessary in life.

One of Mahāvīra's sayings illustrates ahimsa:

> *"All breathing, existing, living, sentient creatures should not be slain, nor treated with violence, nor abused, nor tormented, nor driven away."*

Jainism also insists on the **interconnectedness and interdependence** of all creatures, from humans to invisible organisms, which support each other and cannot live without one another — a deep truth confirmed again and again by scientists studying nature, flora and fauna.

**A Jain story — Rohineya.** Rohineya was an extraordinarily skilled burglar who evaded all attempts to catch him. On his way to a city he accidentally heard a few sentences from a sermon Mahāvīra was giving about achieving liberation from the ordinary life of ignorance. Reaching the city, Rohineya was recognised and arrested; he pretended to be a simple farmer. A minister devised a clever plan to force him to confess his identity, but Rohineya, remembering Mahāvīra's words, detected the plan and defeated it. Feeling remorseful, he approached Mahāvīra, confessed his crimes, returned the stolen treasures, and asked for forgiveness. He became a monk, realised the illusion he had been living in, and focused on acquiring higher knowledge. The story illustrates the importance of right action and right thinking, and that everyone deserves a second chance.

In both Buddhism and Jainism, **ahimsa** means much more than refraining from physical violence against a person or animal — it also means refraining from violence in thought, such as ill feelings towards anyone (sometimes even directed at oneself). Monks, and sometimes nuns, in both traditions travelled across the land to spread their teachings — some founded new monasteries in faraway places, others led ascetic lives in caves cut into rock. Archaeological findings have revealed many traces of such monasteries, sometimes even the names of monks who lived in the rock-cut caves and slept on stone beds.

*Figure (p.119):* caves cut into the rock at **Ellora** (Maharashtra), dating between the 6th and the 10th centuries CE — some Hindu, others Buddhist and Jain.

### 5. Why "schools of thought" rather than "religions"

In English, Hinduism, Buddhism, Jainism and Sikhism are often labelled 'religions'. The textbook deliberately avoids this term, preferring **'schools of thought'** and (for folk/tribal beliefs) **'belief systems'**, because these traditions have many aspects — philosophical, spiritual, religious, ethical, social — and many scholars agree the word 'religion' is too limiting in the Indian context.

### 6. Other schools of thought — Chārvāka

There were yet other schools of thought at the time. One, known as the **'Chārvāka'** school (also 'Lokāyata'), believed that the material world is the only thing that exists, and therefore there is no life after death. This school never gained much popularity and disappeared over time — mentioned here to show the wide diversity of intellectual and spiritual belief systems available; people were free to choose what suited them.

Although the Vedic, Buddhist and Jain schools had important differences, they also shared common concepts — **dharma, karma, rebirth, the search for an end to suffering and ignorance**, and many important values. This is the 'trunk' of the cultural tree the chapter began with.

### 7. Folk and Tribal Roots

The cultural roots covered so far are well documented in texts. India has also had rich **'oral traditions'** — teachings or practices transmitted through everyday practice, without written texts (as with the Vedas originally). Among them are numerous **folk traditions** (transmitted by common people) and **tribal traditions** (transmitted by tribes).

**What is a tribe?** Anthropologists usually define a tribe as a group of families or clans sharing a tradition of common descent, a culture and a language, living as a close-knit community under a chief, holding no private property. Interestingly, ancient India had no word for 'tribe' — tribes were simply different **janas** living in a specific environment, such as forests or mountains. The Constitution of India uses the terms 'tribes' and 'tribal communities' in English, and **janjāti** in Hindi.

According to official figures, in **2011 India had 705 tribes** spread over most states, totalling about **104 million people** — more than the populations of Australia and the United Kingdom combined! In the 19th century, anthropologists studying tribes often described them as 'primitive' or 'inferior' to 'civilised' people. With deeper study of tribal communities and their rich, complex cultures, such biased judgements have mostly been abandoned.

The chapter gives examples of shared folk and tribal beliefs, picking up mid-example: a folk deity in the form of the Moon who later rose to the higher status of a supreme god — this is also the case of the god **Khandoba** in parts of central India. In eastern India, the **Munda and Santhal** tribals, among others, worship **Singbonga**, a supreme deity who created this whole world. There are many more such examples.

The Indian sociologist **André Béteille** summed up this situation:

> *"The thousands of castes and tribes on the Indian subcontinent have influenced each other in their religious beliefs and practices since the beginning of history and before. That the tribal religions have been influenced by Hinduism is widely accepted, but it is equally true that Hinduism, not only in its formative phase but throughout its evolution, has been influenced by tribal religions."*

Clearly, this long interaction resulted in **mutual enrichment**. In this manner, folk and tribal beliefs and practices also count among India's cultural roots. (The textbook notes this point will be developed further in the next chapter.)

**"Before we move on..." — chapter summary bullets (p.122):**

- The Vedas, India's earliest texts, gave rise to several schools of thought. Vedanta and Yoga are among the best known.
- Buddhism and Jainism departed from the authority of the Vedas and laid emphasis on some specific values and practices.
- Although these schools had different principles and methods, they also shared some important concepts; they were all looking for the cause of suffering and the means of removing ignorance.
- Tribal belief systems and art have interacted for millenniums with Hinduism. There was free borrowing and giving from every side. Tribal belief systems generally regard the land and its features as sacred; they often have, at the same time, a higher concept of divinity.

## Important Rules / Formulas

- **ekam sat viprā bahudhā vadanti** — "The Existent is one, sages call it by many names." (Ṛig Veda; core Vedic idea of unity behind many gods)
- **aham brahmāsmi** / **tat tvam asi** — "I am brahman" / "You are That" (Upanishadic identity of ātman with brahman)
- **sarve bhavantu sukhinah** — "May all creatures be happy" (Upanishadic prayer for universal wellbeing)
- The Buddha's teaching: purity comes from truth and dharma within, not from bathing in sacred rivers; conquering oneself surpasses conquering armies.
- Mahāvīra's teaching: no breathing, living, sentient creature should be slain, treated with violence, abused, tormented, or driven away (ahimsa).
- Shared core across Vedic, Buddhist and Jain schools: **dharma, karma, rebirth**, the search for the end of suffering and ignorance.

## Quick Summary

- Indian culture is pictured as an ancient tree: many **roots** feed a common **trunk**, from which many **branches** (art, literature, science, religion, schools of thought, etc.) grow.
- The **four Vedas** (Ṛig, Yajur, Sāma, Atharva) are India's oldest texts — composed orally by rishis and rishikas, transmitted with great precision for millennia; UNESCO recognised Vedic chanting in 2008.
- Vedic society was organised into **janas** (clans); governance clues include *rājā*, *sabha*, *samiti*.
- The **Upanishads** introduced **rebirth**, **karma**, and **Vedanta**'s idea that everything is one essence, **brahman**, identical with the **ātman** (Self). **Yoga** also grew from the Vedas. Together these became the foundations of Hinduism.
- **Buddhism**: founded by **Siddhārtha Gautama** (the Buddha), born in Lumbini, enlightened at Bodh Gaya; taught that **avidyā** (ignorance) and **attachment** cause suffering; founded the **Sangha** of monks and nuns; taught **ahimsa**.
- **Jainism**: founded by **Vardhamāna Mahāvīra**, born near Vaishālī, Bihar; teachings include **ahimsa**, **anekāntavāda**, **aparigraha**.
- Buddhism and Jainism both rejected the authority of the Vedas but shared concepts like **dharma, karma, rebirth** with Vedic thought.
- The **Chārvāka (Lokāyata)** school held there is no life after death; it disappeared over time.
- **Folk and tribal traditions** are oral, non-written roots of Indian culture. India had **705 tribes** (2011), about **104 million people**. Hinduism and tribal religions **mutually influenced** each other (André Béteille).
- The textbook prefers **'schools of thought'** / **'belief systems'** over 'religion' because these traditions span philosophical, spiritual, ethical and social dimensions.
