What this chapter is about

Indian culture is pictured as an ancient tree: many roots feed one common trunk, from which many branches grow. This chapter traces a few of those roots — the Vedas and the schools of thought that grew from them, the new paths of Buddhism and Jainism that emerged in the 1st millennium BCE, and the folk and tribal traditions that shaped Indian culture just as deeply, even though they were never written down.

The twist running through the whole chapter is mutual influence: the Vedic, Buddhist and Jain schools disagreed on method but shared core concepts like karma and rebirth; and, as the sociologist André Béteille put it, Hinduism and tribal religions shaped each other throughout history — not a one-way street.

The six things most worth remembering

1 · Four Vedas, one belief

Ṛig, Yajur, Sāma, Atharva — recited orally for millennia. "ekam sat viprā bahudhā vadanti": one reality, many names.

2 · Brahman ≠ Brahmā

Brahman is Vedanta's impersonal cosmic essence — the ātman (Self) is one with it. Don't confuse it with the god Brahmā.

3 · The Buddha's two causes of suffering

Avidyā (ignorance) and attachment — realised under a pipal tree at Bodh Gaya.

4 · Mahāvīra's three teachings

Ahimsa, anekāntavāda (truth has many sides) and aparigraha (non-possession).

5 · Ahimsa covers thought, not just action

In both Buddhism and Jainism, even ill feelings — towards anyone, including oneself — count as violence.

6 · Mutual enrichment, not one-way

705 tribes, ~104 million people (2011). André Béteille: Hinduism and tribal religions shaped each other.
