  1. The Ṛig Veda is the most recently composed of the four Vedas.
  2. Rishikas were female seers who composed some of the Vedic hymns.
  3. The word 'brahman' refers to the same thing as the god Brahmā.
  4. The Upanishads introduced the concepts of karma and rebirth.
  5. Siddhārtha Gautama left his palace life after seeing an old man, a sick man, a dead body and an ascetic.
  6. Mahāvīra was born in Lumbini.
  7. Anekāntavāda means "non-possession."
  8. In both Buddhism and Jainism, ahimsa includes avoiding violent thoughts, not just violent actions.
  9. The Chārvāka school became the most popular school of thought in ancient India.
  10. According to André Béteille, Hinduism influenced tribal religions but was never influenced by them in return.
Answers with reasons
  1. False — the Ṛig Veda is the oldest (most ancient) of the four, not the most recent.
  2. True — rishis (male) and rishikas (female) together composed the Vedic hymns.
  3. False — brahman is the impersonal divine essence of Vedanta; Brahmā is a separate god. The chapter is explicit that the two must not be confused.
  4. True
  5. True — these four sights led him to give up palace life.
  6. False — Mahāvīra (Vardhamāna) was born near Vaishālī, Bihar. Lumbini was the Buddha's birthplace.
  7. False — anekāntavāda means "not just one aspect" (truth has many sides); aparigraha is the term that means non-possession.
  8. True — ahimsa covers ill feelings and negative thoughts, even towards oneself.
  9. False — the Chārvāka school never gained much popularity and disappeared with time.
  10. False — Béteille's whole point is that the influence was mutual: Hinduism shaped tribal belief and tribal belief shaped Hinduism.

D · Short answers 5 × 2 = 10 marks

D1 What does the mantra "ekam sat viprā bahudhā vadanti" mean, and what does it tell us about the early Vedic worldview?

It means "The Existent [the supreme reality] is one, but sages give it many names." It tells us that the early rishis and rishikas saw the many Vedic gods and goddesses not as separate, unrelated beings, but as different names or aspects of one underlying reality — a worldview centred on unity.

D2 Explain what "ahimsa" means in Buddhism and Jainism, beyond simply "not fighting."

Ahimsa is generally translated as 'non-violence', but originally means 'non-hurting' or 'non-injuring'. In both Buddhism and Jainism it goes well beyond avoiding physical violence against a person or animal — it also means refraining from violence in thought, such as having ill feelings towards anyone, sometimes even towards oneself.

D3 What two new concepts did the Upanishads introduce, building on earlier Vedic ideas?

The Upanishads introduced rebirth (taking birth again and again) and karma (our actions, or the results of our actions). They also developed the idea of brahman and ātman, but these built on the rebirth/karma framework rather than being the very first new ideas mentioned.

D4 Why does the chapter avoid describing Hinduism, Buddhism, Jainism and Sikhism simply as "religions"?

Because these traditions have many aspects at once — philosophical, spiritual, religious, ethical and social — and many scholars feel the word 'religion' is too limiting to capture all of that in the Indian context. The chapter prefers 'schools of thought' or 'belief systems' instead.

D5 What do anthropologists mean by a "tribe"?

A group of families or clans sharing a tradition of common descent, a culture and a language, living as a close-knit community under a chief, and holding no private property. Interestingly, ancient India had no dedicated word for this — tribes were simply different janas living in a specific environment, such as forests or mountains.

E · Long answers 2 × 5 = 10 marks

E1 Compare the lives of Siddhārtha Gautama and Vardhamāna (Mahāvīra) — their birth, the age at which they left home, and what each came to teach.

Birth

Siddhārtha Gautama was born a prince in Lumbini (today in Nepal), around 560 BCE by the textbook's estimate. Vardhamāna was also born into a royal family, in the early 6th century BCE, near the city of Vaishālī in modern-day Bihar.

Leaving home

Siddhārtha left his palace life at 29, after seeing an old man, a sick man, a dead body and a peaceful ascetic on a chariot ride through the city. Vardhamāna left home slightly later, at 30, in search of spiritual knowledge; after 12 years of ascetic discipline he achieved 'infinite knowledge'.

What each came to teach

Siddhārtha became the Buddha ('enlightened/awakened') after meditating at Bodh Gaya. He taught that avidyā (ignorance) and attachment cause suffering, and he founded the Sangha of monks and (later) nuns. Vardhamāna became known as Mahāvīra ('great hero') and taught ahimsa, anekāntavāda (truth has many aspects) and aparigraha (non-possession). Both men taught ahimsa and rejected the authority of the Vedas, but Jainism places special emphasis on non-possession and the many-sidedness of truth, ideas not central to early Buddhism in quite the same form.

E2 Explain the relationship between Hinduism and tribal/folk belief systems as described by André Béteille, using examples from the chapter (Khandoba, Singbonga).

André Béteille, an Indian sociologist, argued that the thousands of castes and tribes on the Indian subcontinent have influenced each other's religious beliefs and practices since the beginning of history and before. He pointed out that while it is widely accepted that tribal religions have been influenced by Hinduism, it is equally true that Hinduism — not only in its formative phase but throughout its evolution — has been influenced by tribal religions. In other words, the influence runs both ways, not just from Hinduism outward.

The chapter gives concrete examples of this exchange: a folk deity in the form of the Moon that rose to the status of a supreme god, and the god Khandoba worshipped in parts of central India, which followed a similar pattern. In eastern India, the Munda and Santhal tribals worship Singbonga, a supreme deity said to have created the whole world. These local, tribal belief systems sit alongside — and have shaped — the broader currents of Hinduism, which is why the chapter concludes that folk and tribal beliefs also count among India's cultural roots, the result of long mutual enrichment rather than a one-way spread.

F · HOTS 2 × 3 = 6 marks

Higher-order thinking. Reason it out rather than recalling a fact.

F1 A student argues: "Since Buddhism and Jainism both rejected the authority of the Vedas, they must have nothing in common with Vedic thought." Is this reasoning correct? Explain using at least two concepts.

The student's reasoning is incorrect. Rejecting the Vedas' authority is not the same as sharing nothing with Vedic ideas. The chapter is explicit that, although the Vedic, Buddhist and Jain schools had important differences in principles and methods, they also shared common concepts.

Two examples: (1) Karma — the idea that our actions have consequences — appears across all three, even though Buddhism and Jainism developed their own frameworks around it rather than accepting Vedic ritual authority. (2) Rebirth and the search for an end to suffering and ignorance are shared goals across Vedic, Buddhist and Jain thought, even though each tradition proposes a different path (Vedanta/Yoga versus the Buddha's Eightfold teachings versus Mahāvīra's ahimsa-centred discipline). So the schools disagree on authority and method, but agree on several underlying concepts and concerns — that shared ground is literally called the 'trunk' of the cultural tree in the chapter.

F2 Someone claims: "Tribal belief systems are simple and separate from mainstream Hinduism — they have never influenced each other." Using André Béteille's argument, explain why this claim is inaccurate.

This claim is inaccurate on two counts. First, it assumes tribal belief systems are 'simple' — but the chapter notes that 19th-century anthropologists who called tribal cultures 'primitive' or 'inferior' have largely been proven wrong by deeper study, which reveals rich and complex tribal art and cosmologies (e.g. Khandoba, Singbonga).

Second, and more directly, the claim that Hinduism and tribal belief "have never influenced each other" contradicts André Béteille's central argument: that castes and tribes on the subcontinent have influenced each other's beliefs and practices since before recorded history, and that this influence runs in both directions — Hinduism shaped by tribal religions just as much as tribal religions were shaped by Hinduism. The chapter calls the result of this long interaction "mutual enrichment," the opposite of two separate, non-interacting systems.
