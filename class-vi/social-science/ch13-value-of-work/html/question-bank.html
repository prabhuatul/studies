  5. Rajesh's ₹400 represents pure profit unrelated to his skill or effort.

  7. According to the chapter, non-economic activities have no real value at all.
  8. A langar serves food only to a select group of paying visitors.
  9. The Swachh Bharat Abhiyan relies on the collective effort of citizens, not just the government.

Answers with reasons
  1. False — she receives a salary, so it is an economic activity, even though she also serves the country.
  2. False — a wage is paid for a specific period of work, while a salary is a fixed regular (usually monthly) payment; they are different.
  3. True — the mangoes are a non-cash portion of his pay, which is exactly what payment in kind means.
  4. True — the chapter states directly that economic activities add value at each stage of the process of transforming something into another form, which is why this is called value addition.
  5. False — the chapter explicitly says the ₹400 is the monetary value of Rajesh's skill, time and effort, not unrelated profit.
  6. True — e.g. Rohan has a paid job and also volunteers; the same is true of Kabir's grandfather and others in the chapter.
  7. False — the chapter explicitly states the value they generate is important in our lives.
  8. False — a langar serves food to every visitor for free, not a select paying group.
  9. True — it is based on the collective efforts of all Indian citizens.
  10. False — the chapter's THINK ABOUT IT box implies these activities still hold real value, even though they are non-economic.

D · Short answers 5 × 2 = 10 marks

D1 What is the difference between economic and non-economic activities?

Economic activities involve money or are performed in exchange for money or money's worth — for example, a business person selling goods, or a lawyer earning a fee. Non-economic activities do not generate income or wealth; they are done out of feelings like gratitude, love, care and respect — for example, parents cooking for their family or someone volunteering their time.

D2 Explain "payment in kind" using Sahil's example.

Payment in kind is a non-cash payment received by a worker for the work they perform. Sahil, a farm labourer, earns a daily wage for tilling a farmer's land using a tractor. He receives part of his payment in cash, and the remaining part in the form of mangoes of equal value — those mangoes are the payment in kind.

D3 Using Rajesh's chair, explain what "value addition" means.

Rajesh buys wood for ₹600 and, using his skill, tools and effort, turns it into a chair that he sells for ₹1,000. The extra ₹400 is not unrelated profit — it is the monetary value of the skill, time and effort Rajesh put into transforming the wood into furniture. This increase in value at each stage of production is called value addition.

D4 What is sevā, and why does the chapter say it matters even though it isn't paid work?

Sevā means selfless service, seen at places like temples, gurudwaras, mosques and churches — for example, langars serving free food to every visitor. The chapter explains that it matters because, even without money involved, sevā fosters a sense of satisfaction and gratitude, and is a way of contributing to society without expecting anything in return — showing that non-economic activities still generate real value.

D5 What do the Swachh Bharat Abhiyan and Van Mahotsav have in common?

Both are examples of the strength of community participation. The Swachh Bharat Abhiyan is based on the collective effort of all Indian citizens to keep their surroundings clean, while Van Mahotsav ('festival of forests') brings communities together for tree plantation drives to promote awareness about the value of trees and forest conservation. Neither involves payment — both rely on people voluntarily working together for a shared, non-economic goal.

E · Long answers 2 × 5 = 10 marks

E1 Using examples from Kavya's family, explain the four different ways people are compensated for economic activities.

Salary

Kavya's uncle, a technician operating a bulldozer at the highway construction site, receives a monthly salary for his services. Kavya's aunt, employed at the village post office, is also paid a monthly salary.

Fee

In the evening, after her post office job, Kavya's aunt conducts online classes to help students prepare for school exams, charging a weekly fee for this professional service.

Wage

Sahil, a farm labourer who uses a tractor to till a farmer's land, earns a daily wage for his efforts — paid for the specific period of work he does each day.

Payment in kind

Part of Sahil's daily wage is paid in cash, but the remaining part is paid in mangoes of equal value — a non-cash payment, which is called payment in kind.

Together, these four examples — all from a single family's story — show that "being paid" can take several different forms, not just a single monthly cheque.

E2 The chapter states: "There is great value attached to people who are engaged in community service activities." Explain and justify this statement using at least two examples from the chapter.

The chapter shows that community service — even though it generates no income — creates real, important value for society. Two clear examples support this:

First, sevā, as practised through langars at gurudwaras (such as the one at the Golden Temple) and prasād distribution at temples, provides free food to every visitor, regardless of who they are or whether they can pay. This fosters satisfaction and gratitude, and is described as contributing to society without expecting anything in return — a value that cannot be measured in rupees, but clearly matters to the people who receive that help.

Second, the Swachh Bharat Abhiyan and Van Mahotsav show that when individuals contribute their time and effort collectively — cleaning streets and parks, or planting trees — the impact scales up to benefit an entire neighbourhood, society or nation, something no single paid worker or transaction could achieve alone. The chapter's closing summary makes this explicit: non-economic activities contribute to social welfare, personal wellbeing, and the overall quality of life — which is precisely why "great value" is attached to the people who give their time to these activities, even without being paid for it.

F · HOTS 2 × 3 = 6 marks

Higher-order thinking. Reason it out rather than recalling a fact.

F1 A student argues: "Since non-economic activities don't earn money, they must be less important than economic activities." Evaluate this claim using the chapter's ideas.

This claim is not well supported by the chapter. The chapter never ranks economic activities above non-economic ones — instead, it treats them as two different kinds of contribution, each valuable in its own way. Economic activities create monetary value (like Rajesh's ₹400 of value addition), which supports livelihoods and allows goods and services to be exchanged. But the chapter is equally direct that non-economic activities "contribute to social welfare, personal wellbeing and enhance the overall quality of life" — outcomes that money cannot fully buy or replace.

Consider that the same people in the chapter — Rohan, Kabir's grandfather, Kavya's aunt — perform both kinds of activity without treating one as more important than the other. If non-economic activities were truly "less important," a langar feeding every visitor for free, or a community coming together for Swachh Bharat Abhiyan, would not be presented as meaningful achievements worth learning about. The chapter's argument is that both kinds of activity are necessary for a functioning, caring society — not that one outranks the other.

F2 Someone claims: "Value addition only applies to people who make physical products, like carpenters or factory workers." Is this true, based on the chapter's definition? Explain your reasoning.

This claim is too narrow. The chapter defines value addition generally, as "the increase in monetary value created at each stage of transforming something into another form" — it uses Rajesh the carpenter as a concrete, easy-to-calculate example (wood into a chair), but the underlying idea is not limited to physical objects.

Consider Kavya's aunt: she is paid a fee for conducting online exam-preparation classes. No physical object is being transformed, but a form of value is still being added — her knowledge, time and teaching skill turn a student's raw effort into better exam preparation, and people are willing to pay for that transformation, just as they pay for a chair. Similarly, the lawyer mentioned earlier in the chapter, who earns a fee for arguing a case, is not producing a physical good, yet is still engaged in an economic activity involving the exchange of skill for money. So while Rajesh's chair is the chapter's clearest numerical illustration, the concept of value addition — turning effort and skill into something people are willing to pay for — applies just as much to services as it does to physical products.
