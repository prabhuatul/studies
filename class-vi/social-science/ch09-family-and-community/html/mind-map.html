How it all fits together

Two units, one idea: the family teaches values through daily life, and the community — rural or urban — is simply families and people supporting each other at a larger scale. Both are ultimately interdependent.

How the ideas connect

Five links worth saying aloud — this is what turns facts into understanding.

1 · Family is community in miniature

Everything the chapter says about community — shared duties, interdependence, support in a crisis — is first taught at home, inside the family, through values like tyāga and sevā.

2 · Sacrifice, not just sharing

Shalini's cotton dress isn't really about clothes — it's tyāga in action. The Bhils' unpaid halma work is the same value, scaled up to a whole community.

3 · Roles adapt, they don't disappear

When Tenzing's mother got busier, his father didn't stop contributing — he took on new chores. Responsibility shifts within the family; it doesn't vanish.

4 · Rural and urban, same principle

Jhabua's halma and Kamal Parmar's street classes look nothing alike, but both are people giving time and effort without expecting anything in return.

5 · No community stands alone

Even a Residents' Welfare Association — seemingly self-contained — depends on traders and municipal workers. Interdependence is the chapter's closing idea for a reason.
