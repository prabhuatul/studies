## Reasons — read these for anything you got wrong

**1.** A — The top
   *A map always presents an area as seen from directly above, looking down on the surface.*

**2.** B — An atlas
   *An atlas is a book or collection of maps. A globe is a sphere with a map drawn on it, which is quite different.*

**3.** C — Physical map
   *Physical maps show natural features. Political maps show countries and boundaries; thematic maps carry one specific kind of information.*

**4.** D — Political map
   *Political maps show details of countries or states — boundaries, cities and capitals.*

**5.** A — Distance, direction and symbols
   *The chapter names exactly three components: distance (handled by the scale), direction (the cardinal points) and symbols.*

**6.** B — 3,000 m
   *Multiply the map distance by the scale: 6 × 500 m = 3,000 m (that is, 3 km).*

**7.** B — 200 km
   *Divide to find the value of one centimetre: 500 ÷ 2.5 = 200 km per cm.*

**8.** B — 4 cm
   *Divide the real length by the scale: 40 m ÷ 10 m per cm = 4 cm.*

**9.** C — North, east, south, west
   *The cardinal directions (or cardinal points) are north, east, south and west. North is at the top of the map and the others follow clockwise.*

**10.** D — Intermediate directions
   *These four lie between the cardinal points and are called intermediate directions. Only N, E, S and W are cardinal.*

**11.** A — Survey of India
   *The Survey of India, a government body, has fixed the standard symbol set for maps of India. Different countries use different sets.*

**12.** B — There is not enough space on a map to draw every feature
   *On the map of a large city or country there simply is not room to draw every building. A symbol packs many details into the limited space available.*

**13.** C — Nearly a sphere, slightly flattened at the poles
   *The Earth is nearly spherical but slightly flattened at the poles — not at the Equator. In practice we treat it as spherical.*

**14.** D — A curved surface cannot be flattened without tearing
   *The peel tears at the edges, proving that a sphere cannot be shown accurately on a flat sheet of paper. That is why we need a globe.*

**15.** A — A sphere on which a map is drawn
   *A globe is a sphere with a map drawn on it — of the Earth, the Moon, Mars, or even the stars and constellations.*

**16.** B — d2 to d4
   *The chapter uses this exact example: the queen's pawn moves from d2 to d4. The letter and number together name each square — a system of coordinates.*

**17.** C — Latitude and longitude
   *Latitude gives the distance from the Equator and longitude the distance from the Prime Meridian. Together they are the coordinates of a place.*

**18.** D — Equator
   *Latitude measures how far you are from the Equator. It is longitude that measures distance from the Prime Meridian.*

**19.** A — 0°
   *The Equator is latitude 0°. The poles are at 90°N and 90°S.*

**20.** B — 90°S
   *The two poles are at 90° North and 90° South, written 90°N and 90°S.*

**21.** C — East and west, parallel to the Equator
   *A parallel of latitude runs east–west, parallel to the Equator, drawing a circle around the Earth. Meridians of longitude are the ones running pole to pole.*

**22.** D — The Equator
   *The Equator is the largest circle on the globe, and it is itself a parallel of latitude. The Prime Meridian is not a parallel at all — it is a meridian.*

**23.** A — Grow smaller
   *The Equator is the largest circle, and the parallels grow smaller as you approach either pole. This is why they are unequal in length.*

**24.** B — Prime Meridian
   *Longitude measures distance from the Prime Meridian, travelling along the Equator. Latitude is what measures distance from the Equator.*

**25.** C — Half-circles running from one pole to the other
   *Every meridian is a half-circle joining the two poles. Parallels of latitude are the full circles.*

**26.** D — All meridians of longitude are equal; parallels are unequal
   *All meridians are half-circles of the same length. Parallels differ — the Equator is longest and they shrink towards the poles.*

**27.** A — London, England
   *Greenwich is an area of London in England. That is why the Prime Meridian is also called the Greenwich Meridian.*

**28.** C — 1884
   *The agreement was made in 1884, fixing the Greenwich meridian as the Prime Meridian at 0° longitude.*

**29.** B — 180°
   *Longitude runs from 0° to 180°, either eastward or westward, with the letter E or W added.*

**30.** B — Because 180°E and 180°W are the same meridian
   *180°E and 180°W are one and the same line, so the letter is dropped and it is noted simply as 180°.*

**31.** C — A grid
   *Together these lines constitute a grid (also called grid lines) over the globe, which is what lets us locate any place precisely.*

**32.** D — Eastern and Western Hemispheres
   *The Prime Meridian, running north–south, splits the Earth into the Western and Eastern Hemispheres. It is the Equator that makes the Northern and Southern.*

**33.** A — Northern and Southern Hemispheres
   *The Equator runs east–west around the middle of the Earth, so it separates the Northern Hemisphere from the Southern.*

**34.** D — Torrid
   *Near the Equator the climate is hot, called "torrid". It becomes "temperate" at middle latitudes and "frigid" near the poles.*

**35.** A — Becomes more moderate, then colder
   *Moving away from the Equator the climate first becomes more moderate ("temperate") and then, closer to the poles, grows colder ("frigid").*

**36.** D — madhya rekhā
   *"madhya rekhā" means "middle line". It was India's prime meridian, used many centuries before Europe fixed on Greenwich.*

**37.** A — Ujjain
   *Ujjayinī is today the city of Ujjain, a renowned centre of astronomy for many centuries.*

**38.** C — 1,500 years ago
   *Varāhamihira lived and worked at Ujjayinī some 1,500 years ago.*

**39.** B — 75.8°E
   *Fig. 1.5 marks the Ujjayinī meridian at 75.8°E. Some ancient cities sit very close to it, others slightly off, because measuring longitude then needed timekeeping that was less precise.*

**40.** C — 24 hours
   *One complete rotation takes 24 hours. This, together with the 360° of a full turn, is what gives us 15° per hour.*

**41.** B — 15°
   *A full turn is 360° and takes 24 hours, so 360 ÷ 24 = 15° per hour. This is the single most useful number in the chapter.*

**42.** D — West to east
   *The Earth spins west to east. That is why places lying to the east see the Sun rise — and set — earlier, and so are ahead in time.*

**43.** D — 2 p.m.
   *30 ÷ 15 = 2 hours. Since 30°E lies east of Greenwich, the time is ahead, so add 2 hours to noon: 2 p.m.*

**44.** C — 2 hours
   *Divide the difference in longitude by 15: 30 ÷ 15 = 2 hours. This is the method used for the Porbandar and Tinsukia example.*

**45.** A — 9 a.m.
   *45 ÷ 15 = 3 hours. Because 45°W lies west of Greenwich the time is behind, so subtract 3 hours from noon: 9 a.m.*

**46.** C — 5 hours 30 minutes
   *IST = GMT + 5 hours 30 minutes (often written 5.5 hours).*

**47.** B — 5:30 p.m.
   *Add IST's offset of 5 hours 30 minutes to 12:00 noon, which gives 5:30 p.m.*

**48.** A — Because it would be inconvenient for a country to use many different local times
   *Every meridian has its own local time, so one country would be running on dozens of slightly different clocks. A single standard time, based on one meridian passing through the country, is far more convenient.*

**49.** D — Tend to follow international borders
   *The lines have to respect each country's standard time, so they tend to follow international borders rather than run exactly along meridians.*

**50.** A — Subtract one day
   *Travelling eastward across the Date Line you subtract a day (Monday becomes Sunday). Travelling westward you add a day.*
