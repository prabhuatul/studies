## D. Short Answers

**1. Scale.** The scale of a map states how a distance on the map relates to the real distance on the ground — for example, 1 cm = 500 m. It is needed because a very large area has to be fitted onto a small sheet of paper, and without the scale we could not work out real distances from the map.

**2. Orange-peel activity.** Peel an orange so that you have just three or four large pieces of skin, then try to flatten them on a table. You cannot do it without tearing them at the edges. This shows that the curved surface of a sphere cannot be shown accurately on a flat sheet of paper — which is why a globe represents the Earth better than a flat map.

**3. Parallels versus meridians.** Any two of:
- Parallels of latitude run **east–west**; meridians of longitude run **north–south** (pole to pole).
- Parallels are **full circles**; meridians are **half-circles**.
- Parallels are **unequal** in length (the Equator is the longest and they shrink towards the poles); meridians are **all equal** in length.
- Latitude is measured from the Equator (0°–90°); longitude is measured from the Prime Meridian (0°–180°).

**4. Dividing the Earth.** The **Prime Meridian** divides the Earth into the **Western Hemisphere** and the **Eastern Hemisphere**. The **Equator** divides it into the **Northern Hemisphere** and the **Southern Hemisphere**.

**5. Multiple time zones.** Some countries stretch so far from east to west that a single standard time would leave the clock badly out of step with the Sun at one end. So they are divided into several zones. The **USA has six** time zones and **Russia has 11** — crossing Russia from east to west means resetting your watch 10 times.

## E. Long Answers

**1. Coordinates (5 marks)**

*The market.* Imagine a big market with neat rows of identical shops. To help a friend find you, you say: "Meet me at 6 pm at the 7th shop in the 5th row from the entrance." Two numbers — the row and the shop — fix the position exactly.

*The chessboard.* On a chessboard, letters **a to h** run along one side and numbers **1 to 8** along the other, so every square has a unique name. When White opens by pushing the queen's pawn two squares forward, the move is recorded as **d2 to d4** — again, a letter and a number together identify each square.

*On the Earth.* The same principle is used on maps and globes. **Latitude** gives the distance north or south of the Equator, and **longitude** gives the distance east or west of the Prime Meridian. Taken together these two values are the **coordinates** of a place, and they pin down any point on the Earth's surface. For example, Delhi lies at about **29°N latitude and 77°E longitude**. All the parallels and meridians together form a **grid** over the globe, which is what makes this precision possible.

**2. Longitude and time (5 marks)**

*Why 15° per hour.* The Earth spins on its axis from **west to east**, completing one full turn every **24 hours**. A full turn is **360°**. Dividing 360° by 24 hours gives **15° per hour** — so every 15° of longitude corresponds to exactly one hour of time.

*How local time changes.* Because the Earth turns eastward, places to the east meet the Sun earlier. So moving **eastward** from the Prime Meridian, you **add** an hour for every 15°: if it is 12 noon at Greenwich, it is 1 pm at 15°E and 2 pm at 30°E. Moving **westward**, you **subtract**: 11 am at 15°W, 10 am at 30°W. This is why measuring a place's longitude also tells us the time there.

*Why standard time is needed.* If every meridian kept its own local time, a single country would be running on dozens of slightly different clocks — hopelessly inconvenient for railways, offices and daily life. So most countries adopt one **standard time**, based on a meridian passing through the country. India uses **Indian Standard Time (IST)**, which is **5 hours 30 minutes ahead of GMT**. These standard times are grouped into **time zones**, which broadly follow the 15° bands but bend to follow **international borders** so that a country is not split across two times.

## F. HOTS

**1.** The student is **wrong**. Local time depends **only on longitude**, not on latitude. Since both cities lie on the same meridian, the Sun reaches its highest point over both at the same moment, so their local time is **identical**.

What *would* differ is their **climate**, and the **length of their days and seasons**. The city at 20°N is nearer the Equator, so its climate is hotter (closer to 'torrid'), while the city at 60°N is much nearer the pole and has a colder, more 'frigid' climate. This is the same point made by the textbook's Delhi–Bengaluru question: those two cities share a longitude of 77°E, so there is practically no difference in local time despite their latitudes being 29°N and 13°N.

**2.** The **traveller is correct**. The rule for crossing the International Date Line is that travelling **eastward you subtract a day**. So crossing on a Friday going east makes it **Thursday** — she genuinely does go back a day, even though she has been flying forwards in time on her watch.

Had she flown in the opposite direction — **westward** across the Date Line — she would have had to **add** a day, so Thursday would have become Friday. This is why the **+12 and −12 time zones meet** at that line: they show the same clock time but are a full day apart on the calendar.
