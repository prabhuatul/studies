Locating Places on Earth

This chapter teaches how we represent the Earth and pinpoint any place on it — from a small city map all the way to the International Date Line.

Core Concepts

1 A map and its components

A map is a drawing of some area as seen from above. It can cover a small area (a village, a town), a bigger one (a district or state), or a very large one (India, or the whole world).

There are three important components of every map:

  1. Distance — handled through the scale
  2. Direction — handled through the cardinal points
  3. Symbols — handled through a standard symbol set
A map is like a treasure guide: it shows you where things are and how to get to them.
2 Distance and scale

A huge place fits onto a small sheet of paper thanks to the map’s scale. Each centimetre on the map stands for a fixed distance on the ground.

  • If the scale is 1 cm = 500 m, then 3 cm on the map means 1,500 m on the ground.
  • On the map of India (Fig. 5.2), the scale is drawn as a ruler with ‘500’ above it and ‘km’ beside it. That ruler measures 2.5 cm in print and represents 500 km on the ground.

The actual distance between two points on a map therefore depends entirely on the scale being used.

3 Direction

North is at the top of the map. Moving clockwise from north: east, south, west. These four are the cardinal directions (or cardinal points).

Between them lie the intermediate directions: northeast, southeast, southwest, northwest. Most maps simply carry an arrow marked ‘N’ pointing north.

   Northwest  |  Northeast
            \ | /
    West ---- + ---- East
            / | \
   Southwest  |  Southeast
           South
4 Symbols

On the map of a large city or a country there is not enough room to draw every building. Instead a symbol stands for each feature — buildings (railway station, school, post office), roads and railway lines, and natural elements (a river, a pond, a forest). This packs a great many details into a small space.

Different countries use different symbol sets. In India the Survey of India, a government body, has fixed the standard set.

Abbreviations to remember PO = Post Office · PTO = Post & Telegraph Office · PS = Police Station
5 Mapping the Earth — why a globe

Mapping the Earth is harder than mapping a town, because the Earth is not flat — it is nearly a sphere. (‘Nearly’, because it is slightly flattened at the poles; in practice we treat it as spherical.)

A sphere cannot be shown accurately on a flat sheet of paper. Try the orange-peel test: peel an orange into three or four large pieces and flatten them on a table — you cannot do it without tearing them at the edges.

A globe is a sphere with a map drawn on it. Because the globe and the Earth share the same spherical shape, a globe represents the Earth’s geography better than a flat map does.

6 Understanding coordinates

Two everyday examples show the idea:

  • The market. “Meet me at 6 pm at the 7th shop in the 5th row from the entrance.” Two numbers fix the shop exactly.
  • The chessboard. Letters a–h run along one side and numbers 1–8 along the other, so every square has a name. When White opens by pushing the queen’s pawn two squares forward, the pawn is said to have moved from d2 to d4.

In both cases two values together pin down one position. This is a system of coordinates, and the same idea is used on maps to locate any place on Earth.

7 Latitudes

Spin the globe: the two fixed points are the poles, and halfway between them runs the Equator.

Stand on the Equator and walk towards a pole — your distance from the Equator grows. Latitude measures that distance. At any point on the journey you can draw an imaginary line running east and west, parallel to the Equator; this is a parallel of latitude, and it forms a circle around the Earth.

  • The Equator is the largest of these circles.
  • The parallels grow smaller as you move north or south.
  • Latitudes are given in degrees: the Equator is 0°, the North Pole 90°N, the South Pole 90°S.
Latitude and climate Near the Equator the climate is generally hot (‘torrid’). As latitude increases towards a pole the climate becomes more moderate (‘temperate’). Closer to the poles it grows cold (‘frigid’).
8 Longitudes

Travel from the North Pole to the South Pole by the shortest line. You could go through Europe and Africa, or equally through Asia — the distance is the same. These lines are the meridians of longitude, and they are all half-circles running pole to pole.

Why longitude gives us time

The Earth spins on its axis. Imagine a desk lamp as the Sun shining on a globe: as the globe turns eastward, it is morning in some places, midday, evening or night in others — breakfast time in one country is lunchtime in another while a third is fast asleep. So measuring a place’s longitude also measures the time there.

The reference line

Longitude is measured from the Prime Meridian, marked 0°. It is also called the Greenwich Meridian because in 1884 some nations agreed that the meridian through Greenwich, an area of London in England, would be the international standard.

  • Longitude increases from 0° to 180° going either west or east, with ‘W’ or ‘E’ added.
  • Examples in round figures: New York 74°W, Delhi 77°E, Tokyo 140°E.
  • 180°W and 180°E are the same meridian, so it is written simply as 180°.

Coordinates. Latitude and longitude together are the two coordinates of a place — “Delhi lies at 29°N latitude and 77°E longitude” (rounded, not exact).

9 The grid and the hemispheres

All the parallels and meridians together form a grid over the globe (also called grid lines). This grid is what lets us define any location precisely.

  • The Prime Meridian divides the Earth into the Western and Eastern Hemispheres.
  • The Equator divides it into the Northern and Southern Hemispheres.
10 India’s own prime meridian

The Greenwich Meridian was not the first prime meridian. Many centuries before Europe, India had its own, called madhya rekhā (‘middle line’). It passed through the city of Ujjayinī (today Ujjain), a renowned centre of astronomy for many centuries. The famous astronomer Varāhamihira lived and worked there some 1,500 years ago.

Indian astronomers already understood latitude and longitude, including the need for a zero meridian, and the Ujjayinī meridian became the reference for calculations in all Indian astronomical texts.

11 Understanding time zones

The Earth rotates west to east, making a full turn every 24 hours. A full turn is 360°, so the Earth turns 15° every hour (15 × 24 = 360).

Mark meridians every 15° eastward from the Prime Meridian — 0°, 15°E, 30°E, 45°E … up to 180°E. Each step is the same as adding one hour of local time:

12 Local time versus standard time

Every meridian has its own local time, but it would be hopelessly inconvenient for one country to run on many local times. So most countries adopt a single standard time, based on a meridian passing through the country.

The number to memorise Indian Standard Time (IST) is 5 hours 30 minutes (5.5 hours) ahead of GMT.

These standard times are organised into time zones, which broadly follow the 15° bands. But on the world map the dividing lines are not straight — they bend to respect each country’s standard time and so tend to follow international borders.

The number written inside a country on a time-zone map is the hours to be added to GMT (positive sign) or subtracted from GMT (negative sign).

Not one zone per country Some countries are simply too large: the USA has six time zones and Russia has 11 — crossing Russia from east to west you must reset your watch 10 times.
13 The International Date Line

Opposite the Prime Meridian, at approximately 180° longitude, lies the International Date Line. The +12 and −12 time zones touch each other along it.

Cross it by ship or plane and you change the date:

Direction of travelDate change
EastwardSubtract a day (Monday → Sunday)
WestwardAdd a day (Sunday → Monday)

It is only approximately at 180°, because it deviates in places to avoid splitting a country into two different dates.

Important Rules & Formulas

  • Rotation rate: 360° ÷ 24 hours = 15° per hour, i.e. 1° = 4 minutes of time
  • Time difference between two places: difference in longitude ÷ 15 = difference in hours
  • Going east → time is ahead (add). Going west → time is behind (subtract).
  • IST = GMT + 5 hours 30 minutes
  • Real distance = map distance × scale factor
  • Latitude range: 0° (Equator) to 90°N / 90°S. Longitude range: 0° (Prime Meridian) to 180° E/W.
  • Parallels of latitude are full circles and unequal in length (Equator largest). Meridians of longitude are half-circles and all equal in length.
  • Crossing the IDL: eastward → subtract a day; westward → add a day.

Solved Examples

1 Local time from longitude

It is 12 noon at Greenwich. What is the local time at 45°E?

  1. Difference in longitude from the Prime Meridian = 45°
  2. Time difference = 45 ÷ 15 = 3 hours
  3. 45°E lies east of Greenwich, so the time is ahead → add 3 hours
  4. 12 noon + 3 hours = 3 pm
2 The Porbandar and Tinsukia puzzle

Two friends talk on the phone in the late afternoon. The one in Tinsukia (Assam) says the sun has set and it is dark; the one in Porbandar (Gujarat) says it is still full daylight. Why? Taking the difference in longitude as 30°, what is the difference in local time?

  1. Assam lies far to the east of Gujarat. The Earth spins west to east, so eastern places see the Sun rise — and set — earlier.
  2. Difference in local time = 30 ÷ 15 = 2 hours
  3. So Tinsukia’s local time is about 2 hours ahead of Porbandar’s. Sunset there happens roughly 2 hours earlier, which is why it is already dark in Assam while Gujarat still has daylight.
  4. Both, however, set their clocks to the same IST — which is exactly why standard time differs from local time.
3 Why 5:30 pm in India at noon in London

IST is defined as GMT + 5 hours 30 minutes. When it is 12:00 noon GMT in London, Indian clocks read 12:00 + 5:30 = 5:30 pm.

4 Delhi and Bengaluru

Delhi is at 29°N, Bengaluru at 13°N; both are at about 77°E. What is the difference in their local time?

  1. Local time depends only on longitude, not latitude.
  2. Both cities share almost the same longitude (77°E), so the difference in longitude ≈ 0°.
  3. Difference in local time = 0 ÷ 15 = practically nil.
  4. Their very different latitudes affect climate, not time.
5 Using a scale

A map has the scale 2.5 cm = 500 km. Two river estuaries are 7.5 cm apart on the map. What is the real distance?

  1. 2.5 cm = 500 km, so 1 cm = 500 ÷ 2.5 = 200 km
  2. Real distance = 7.5 × 200 = 1,500 km

Quick Summary

  • A map is a drawing of an area seen from above; a collection of maps is an atlas. Maps are physical, political or thematic.
  • Every map has three components: distance (scale), direction (cardinal points) and symbols (Survey of India set for India).
  • The Earth is nearly spherical, slightly flattened at the poles, so a flat sheet cannot show it accurately — hence the globe.
  • Latitude measures distance from the Equator (0°) up to the poles (90°N, 90°S). Parallels of latitude are full circles, unequal in length, the Equator being the longest.
  • Longitude measures distance from the Prime Meridian (0°, through Greenwich, fixed in 1884) up to 180° E or W. Meridians are half-circles, all of equal length.
  • Together, latitude and longitude form a grid and give every place its coordinates.
  • The Prime Meridian creates the Eastern/Western hemispheres; the Equator creates the Northern/Southern.
  • India’s ancient prime meridian, madhya rekhā, ran through Ujjayinī (Ujjain) at about 75.8°E; Varāhamihira worked there ~1,500 years ago.
  • The Earth turns 15° per hour, so longitude fixes time. Local time changes with every meridian; countries adopt one standard time instead. IST = GMT + 5:30.
  • Time zones broadly follow 15° bands but bend to follow international borders. Large countries have several (USA 6, Russia 11).
  • The International Date Line lies near 180°, opposite the Prime Meridian. Crossing it eastward you subtract a day; westward you add a day.
  • Latitude also shapes climate: torrid near the Equator, temperate in the middle, frigid near the poles.
