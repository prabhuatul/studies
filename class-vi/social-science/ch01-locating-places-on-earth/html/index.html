What this chapter is about

The whole chapter answers one question: how do we say exactly where something is? It answers it twice — first for a small area, using a map, and then for the whole planet, using the grid of latitudes and longitudes.

The clever twist at the end is that the grid gives us time as well as place. Because the Earth turns a steady 15° every hour, knowing how far east or west you are is knowing what time it is — and that leads to local time, standard time, time zones and the International Date Line.

The six things most worth remembering

1 · Three components of a map

Distance (the scale), direction (the cardinal points) and symbols (fixed for India by the Survey of India).

2 · Why we need a globe

The Earth is nearly a sphere, flattened at the poles. A curved surface cannot be flattened without tearing — the orange-peel test.

3 · Latitude vs longitude

Parallels are full circles of unequal length; meridians are half-circles, all equal. Latitude 0°–90°, longitude 0°–180°.

4 · 15° per hour

360° ÷ 24 hours = 15° every hour. Go east, time is ahead; go west, time is behind.

5 · IST = GMT + 5:30

Which is exactly why it is 5:30 p.m. in India when it is noon in London.

6 · India's own prime meridian

The madhya rekhā through Ujjayinī (Ujjain), where Varāhamihira worked some 1,500 years ago.
