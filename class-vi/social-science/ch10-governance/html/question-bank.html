B · True or false 10 marks

  1. Governance means the same thing as "holding an election".
  2. The executive includes the head of state, the ministers, and law-enforcement agencies.
  3. The judiciary can only punish criminals — it can never examine whether a law itself is fair.
  4. Separation of powers means the three organs of government never interact with each other at all.
  5. India's government operates at only two levels: local and national.
  6. At the State level, the Governor is the actual, full-power executive head.
  7. Dr. Kalam served as President of India before he became a scientist.
  8. Dr. Kalam's story shows that a "nominal" role cannot have any real impact on people's lives.
  9. India is described in the chapter as a representative democracy.
  10. A class voting by show of hands between two picnic spots is an example of direct democracy.
Answers with reasons
  1. False — governance is the broader process of decision-making, rule-organising and enforcement; elections are just one part of a democratic system.
  2. True — the executive implements the laws, and includes the head of state, ministers, and agencies like the cyber police.
  3. False — the judiciary can also examine whether an executive decision or a law passed by the legislature is right or fair.
  4. False — the three organs are kept separate but still interact with each other and work together.
  5. False — India operates at three levels: local, State, and Central (Union).
  6. False — the Governor is a nominal head; the Chief Minister is the actual executive head of the State.
  7. False — Dr. Kalam was already a renowned scientist (the 'Missile Man of India') before becoming the 11th President from 2002 to 2007.
  8. False — the chapter's whole point is the opposite: even a nominal role can have an important real impact, as Dr. Kalam showed.
  9. True — like any modern democracy, India is described as a representative democracy.
  10. True — this is exactly the chapter's example of direct democracy, where every student's opinion is taken to finalise the decision.

D · Short answers 5 × 2 = 10 marks

D1 What is the difference between the legislature, the executive and the judiciary?

The legislature makes (and sometimes updates or removes) laws. The executive implements those laws — this includes the head of state, ministers, and agencies responsible for enforcing law and order. The judiciary is the system of courts that decides whether someone has broken the law, what should be done about it, and can also examine whether a law or an executive decision is fair.

D2 Why is "separation of powers" important?

It keeps the three organs of government separate, although they still interact and work together. This provides a system of checks and balances, where each organ can check what another is doing and restore balance if one organ acts beyond its expected role — preventing any single group from holding all the power.

D3 Using the flood example, explain how India's three levels of government share responsibility.

If a flood is not too severe and affects only part of a district, local authorities may be able to deal with it themselves. If it involves several towns and many villages, the State Government steps in and sends rescue teams. But if it is a massive flood affecting vast areas, the Central Government may also come to help, by sending relief supplies, the army, and so on — each level stepping in according to the scale of the problem.

D4 What does it mean to say the President of India and the State Governors are "nominal" heads?

It means that, in name, they are the heads of the Central and State governments (the President is also the Supreme Commander of the Armed Forces), but they are not the actual executive heads making day-to-day decisions — that role belongs to the Prime Minister (Centre) and Chief Minister (State). Nominal heads do hold certain powers under special circumstances, but normally do not interfere in the government's affairs.

D5 What is the difference between direct democracy and representative democracy, with an example of each?

In direct democracy, every person's own opinion is taken directly to reach a decision — as in the chapter's example of a class voting by a show of hands between two picnic spots. In representative democracy, people vote for representatives (like MLAs or MPs), who then discuss laws, problems and solutions on their behalf in assemblies. India is a representative democracy.

E · Long answers 2 × 5 = 10 marks

E1 Using Fig. 10.5, compare the structure of government at the National level and the State level.

Judiciary

At the national level, the highest court is the Supreme Court of India; at the State level, it is the High Court.

Legislature

At the national level, there are two Houses of Parliament — the Lok Sabha and the Rajya Sabha — which formulate national laws. At the State level, there is usually one State Assembly or Vidhan Sabha (a few States have two).

Executive

The Central Government is led by the President (a nominal head and Supreme Commander of the Armed Forces), with the Prime Minister as the executive head. The State Government is led by the Governor (nominal head), with the Chief Minister as the executive head.

Functions

The Central Government's executive handles matters like Defence, Foreign Affairs, Atomic Energy, Communications, Currency, Interstate Commerce and the formulation of national policies. The State Government's executive handles police and law and order, adapting and implementing Central laws at the State level, public health, agriculture, irrigation and local government. Education is a responsibility shared by both levels.

E2 Using Dr. A.P.J. Abdul Kalam's life and his own quotations, explain what he teaches us about the value of a "nominal" role and about not giving up.

Dr. Kalam, born in a humble family in Rameswaram, Tamil Nadu, in 1931, became a renowned scientist — nicknamed the 'Missile Man of India' — before serving as the 11th President of India from 2002 to 2007. His presidency was, in the chapter's terms, a nominal role, meaning he was not the government's actual day-to-day executive decision-maker. Yet the chapter states plainly that he "could play an important role, impacting countless lives" — he stayed connected to ordinary people and to youth in particular, through his passion for education and innovation, showing that formal, limited power and real influence are not the same thing.

His quotations reinforce a philosophy of persistence and ambition rather than status: "F.A.I.L." becomes "First Attempt In Learning," "E.N.D." becomes "Effort Never Dies," and a "no" becomes "Next Opportunity." He describes a dream not as something seen while sleeping but as "something that does not let you sleep" — framing ambition as active and demanding, not passive. And his final quoted thought ties it together: that a great aim, knowledge, hard work and perseverance — not one's official title — are what let a person achieve anything and make a lasting impact, nominal position or not.

F · HOTS 2 × 3 = 6 marks

Higher-order thinking. Reason it out rather than recalling a fact.

F1 A student argues: "If the President and the Governor don't actually run the government day to day, their roles are pointless and could be removed." Evaluate this argument using the chapter's ideas.

This argument is weak for at least two reasons drawn from the chapter. First, "nominal" does not mean "powerless" — the chapter explicitly notes that nominal heads "do have certain powers under special circumstances," suggesting they serve as a check or safeguard precisely because they normally stay out of day-to-day affairs, stepping in only when genuinely needed. This is consistent with the chapter's broader theme of checks and balances: separating who holds ceremonial/constitutional authority from who makes daily executive decisions is itself a form of distributing power, not wasting it.

Second, Dr. A.P.J. Abdul Kalam's example directly contradicts the idea that a nominal role is "pointless." As President — a nominal role — he still inspired millions of people, especially the youth, through his focus on education and innovation, showing that influence and impact do not depend solely on having full executive power. Removing the position because it lacks day-to-day executive authority would ignore both its constitutional checking function and the kind of national, symbolic and motivational leadership Dr. Kalam demonstrated it can provide.

F2 Someone claims: "A country with a strong representative democracy doesn't need grassroots democracy — elected MPs and MLAs are already enough." Do you agree? Use the chapter's definitions to explain your answer.

This claim oversimplifies what the chapter presents as two different, complementary layers of participation. Representative democracy solves the practical problem that "not all people can actually rule" at once — exactly like a class electing one monitor to speak to the principal instead of the whole class going. MLAs and MPs are elected precisely so that large-scale decisions at the State and national level can be made without requiring the literal participation of every single citizen in every decision.

But the chapter defines grassroots democracy separately, as a system that "enables and encourages the participation of ordinary citizens" — described as the base of the government pyramid (Fig. 10.4), where citizens can have a say in decisions that affect them directly. This matters because representative democracy operates at a distance: an MP or MLA cannot personally know every local concern in their constituency. Grassroots democracy is what keeps ordinary people's voices active between elections and at the local level, where decisions most directly affect daily life — for example, how local governments handle issues close to a particular town or village. So rather than being redundant, grassroots democracy fills a gap that representative democracy, by its very design (delegating decisions to elected representatives), cannot fully cover on its own.
