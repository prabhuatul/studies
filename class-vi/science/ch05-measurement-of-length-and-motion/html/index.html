What this chapter is about

Deepa, an eleven-year-old from Haryana, needs a new uniform — and wonders why the tailor uses a tape, the shopkeeper a rod, and her mother says char angula. With her friends Anish, Hardeep, Padma and Tasneem, she discovers that body-part units (handspan, arm, stride) give different answers for different people, which is why the world adopted standard SI units — the metre. The chapter teaches the correct way to measure (placing the scale, eye position, broken scales, curved lines), how to write units, and then moves to position: the reference point, what motion and rest really mean, and the types of motion — linear, circular, oscillatory and periodic.

The six things most worth remembering

1 · Body parts don't agree

Five friends measured the same table as 13, 14, "slightly more than 13"… because handspans differ. A length = number + unit.

2 · SI unit of length = metre (m)

1 km = 1000 m · 1 m = 100 cm · 1 cm = 10 mm. A 15-cm scale can measure down to 1 mm.

3 · Measure correctly

Scale in contact along the length; eye directly above the point; broken scale → start at a full mark and subtract; curved line → thread or flexible tape.

4 · Reference point

A fixed object or point from which distance is stated — the bus stand, Delhi on a kilometre stone.

5 · Motion vs rest

An object is in motion if its position changes with respect to a reference point with time; otherwise it is at rest. It depends on the reference point you choose.

6 · Three types of motion

Linear (straight line), circular (circular path), oscillatory (to and fro). Circular and oscillatory are periodic.
