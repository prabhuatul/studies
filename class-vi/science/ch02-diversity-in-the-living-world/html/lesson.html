Diversity in the Living World

The nature walk, grouping plants and animals, leaf venation, root types, dicots and monocots, adaptations, habitats, and protecting biodiversity.

Core Concepts

1 The nature walk — diversity all around us

After a refreshing rain, the science teacher Madam Sulekha invites Dr Raghu (a scientist at a nearby Research Laboratory) and Maniram chacha (an elderly person from a nearby community, expert at mimicking bird calls and identifying plants and animals) to lead a nature walk.

  • Objective: to experience the beauty and variety of plants and animals in nature. Students carry a notebook, a pen and a water bottle.
  • Dr Raghu: notice the variety of smells; respect all living creatures and observe them without disturbing.
  • Maniram chacha: not only observe but also listen carefully to different sounds.
  • Students see grasses, bushes, large trees, birds, butterflies moving flower to flower, monkeys jumping between trees.
  • Each bird has a unique chirp — an example of diversity in nature.
2 2.1 Diversity in plants and animals around us

Plants show a variety of features: tall/short, hard/soft stems; different shapes of leaves and their arrangement; flowers varying in colour, shape and scent.

Animals differ in where they live, what they eat and how they move. Some live on land, some on trees (birds), fish in water, and frogs on land as well as in water.

The variety of plants and animals found in a particular region contributes to the biodiversity of that region. Each member has a different role — trees give food and shelter to birds and other animals; animals spread seeds after eating fruits. So plants and animals are dependent on each other.

3 2.2 Why group plants and animals?

Just as arranging books in groups organises a school bag, we can group living things by similarities and differences. Different students may choose different bases — height of plants, presence/absence of flowers, hard/soft stem; for animals, what they eat, where they live, colour, how they move (Fig. 2.2).

Why it mattersGrouping makes it easier to understand and study plants and animals on the basis of their similarities and differences.

5 Leaf venation — reticulate and parallel

The thin lines on a leaf are veins; their pattern is venation.

  • Reticulate venation: a net-like pattern on both sides of a thick middle vein — e.g. hibiscus.
  • Parallel venation: veins run parallel — e.g. banana and grasses.
6 Roots — taproot and fibrous roots
  • Taproot: one main root with small side roots arising from it — e.g. mustard, hibiscus.
  • Fibrous roots: a bunch of similar-sized thin roots arising from the base of the stem — e.g. common grass.

Activity 2.6 reminds us to replant the herbs after observing their roots.

7 The link: venation ↔ root type ↔ seed

Generally, plants with reticulate venation have taproots, while those with parallel venation have fibrous roots. (Sadabahar: taproot + reticulate. Lemongrass: fibrous + parallel. Chickpea: taproot + reticulate. Wheat: fibrous + parallel.)

Seeds: a soaked chickpea splits into two parts — each part is a cotyledon. Plants with two cotyledons are dicotyledons (dicots). Maize has a single thin cotyledon — a monocotyledon (monocot).

9 2.3 Plants and animals in different surroundings

Fish live in water and have streamlined bodies and fins; goats live in grassy areas and move with legs.

  • Cactus (deserts of Rajasthan, seen by Alex): thick, fleshy stems store water to tolerate heat. Hot deserts are very hot by day and very cold at night, with little water.
  • Deodar (Himalayas of Himachal Pradesh, seen by Maya): conical shape, flexible sloping branches let snow slide off easily.
  • Rhododendron: in the Shola forests of the Nilgiris they are shorter with smaller leaves to survive heavy winds on mountain tops; in Sikkim (Pema) they are taller. The same plant shows different features in different regions.
  • Sea: Sagar saw huge whales and colourful fish in the Andaman and Nicobar Islands — a streamlined body helps fish swim.

Biodiversity varies from region to region because of diverse conditions.

Kashi from Rajasthan adds: camels excrete small amounts of urine, have dry dung and do not sweat — so they lose little water and can survive for many days without drinking water.

11 Adaptations and habitats

The special features that enable plants and animals to survive in a particular region are called adaptations. E.g. the shape of the deodar and the height of the rhododendron.

The place where plants and animals live is called their habitat. Sea turtle → sea/ocean; camel → hot or cold desert; rhododendron → mountains. A habitat provides food, water, air, shelter and other needs; many living things can share one habitat. Habitat shapes the biodiversity of a region.

  • Terrestrial (land): forests, deserts, grasslands, mountains.
  • Aquatic (water): ponds, lakes, rivers, oceans.
  • Amphibians (e.g. frogs) live in water as well as on land.
12 Loss and protection of biodiversity

When habitats are damaged, plants and animals lose their homes, food and other resources — leading to loss of biodiversity.

  • The Bengal Tiger, Cheetah and Great Indian Bustard have declined due to habitat loss caused by human activities.
  • Project Tiger — started 1973 to protect the Bengal Tiger.
  • Cheetah Reintroduction Project — started 2022.
  • Great Indian Bustard habitats declared Protected Areas in Gujarat, Rajasthan and Maharashtra.
  • Sacred groves — undisturbed forest patches all over India, protected by local communities; home to many medicinal plants — "a community protected treasure of biodiversity".
Success story — Save Silent ValleyA moist evergreen forest in Palakkad district, Kerala, was saved by a movement of common people against a proposed hydroelectric dam across the Kunthipuzha river. The battle lasted 10 years — awareness programmes, letters to editors, newspaper articles, seminars, petitions and court appeals.
13 Know a scientist — Janaki Ammal and Salim Ali

Janaki Ammal (1897–1984) — Indian botanist who documented and preserved India's plant biodiversity; played a key role in the 'Save Silent Valley' movement; as head of the Botanical Survey of India, she started programmes to document India's plant diversity.

Salim Ali (1896–1987) — the 'Birdman of India'. Travelled across India observing birds, listing them and documenting their travel routes and habitats; helped preserve Keoladeo National Park (Bharatpur, Rajasthan) and Ranganathittu Bird Sanctuary (Mandya, Karnataka); wrote a landmark series of 10 books on birds of the Indian Subcontinent; awarded the Padma Vibhushan in 1976.

Important Rules & Facts

  • Tree: tall + thick woody stem + branches high up. Shrub: many woody stems + branches near ground. Herb: small + soft green stem.
  • Reticulate venation = net-like; parallel venation = veins run side by side.
  • Dicot → 2 cotyledons → reticulate venation → taproot.
  • Monocot → 1 cotyledon → parallel venation → fibrous roots.
  • Adaptation = special feature to survive in a region. Habitat = where a living thing lives.
  • Terrestrial = land; aquatic = water; amphibian = both.
  • Habitat damage → loss of homes, food and resources → loss of biodiversity.

Activities in the chapter

2.1 Activity 2.1 — Let us explore and record (nature walk)

Plan a nature walk; observe plants, insects, birds and other animals, and the weather. Collect fallen leaves/flowers for a scrapbook — but do not pluck or disturb. Record stems, leaves and flowers in Table 2.1 (e.g. grass: soft, thin stem, single leaves growing alternately; tulsi: hard, thin stem, pairs of opposite leaves, pinkish-purple flowers; hibiscus: hard stem; neem: hard, thick stem, smooth leaves). Record animals' homes, food and movement in Table 2.2 (crow: tree, insects, flies and walks; ant: nest in soil, leaves/seeds/insects, six legs; cow: grass and leaves).

2.2 Activity 2.2 — Let us appreciate

Close your eyes for 30 seconds, think of a plant and an animal you have appreciated, and draw them on the blackboard. Count how many different ones the whole class drew — and realise there are many more varieties: biodiversity.

2.3 Activity 2.3 — Let us group (picture cards)

In groups of 5–6, paste pictures of plants and animals on cards, then group them by common features and compare the basis of grouping with other groups. Different groups choose different bases.

2.4 Activity 2.4 — Let us group (height and stem)

Observe plant height (shorter/as tall as/taller than you), stem colour, bendability, thickness, and where branches arise. Fill Table 2.3: mango — tall, brown, hard, thick, branches high → tree; rose — medium, brown, hard, thin, branches near ground → shrub; tomato — short, green, tender, thin → herb.

2.5 Activity 2.5 — Let us compare (leaves)

Compare the shape and veins of collected leaves: hibiscus (reticulate) vs banana and grass (parallel).

2.6 Activity 2.6 — Let us find out (roots)

Use a khurpi (trowel) to dig out small herbs without damaging roots (wet the soil first), wash and observe the roots, then replant them. Compare the mustard (taproot) and grass (fibrous) root systems.

2.7 Activity 2.7 — Let us relate and analyse

Before planting saplings (lemongrass, marigold, sadabahar…), record leaf venation and root type in Table 2.4. Lemongrass: parallel + fibrous. Sadabahar: reticulate + taproot. Conclusion: generally reticulate ↔ taproot, parallel ↔ fibrous.

2.8 Activity 2.8 — Let us compare (seeds)

Soak chickpea and maize seeds for two or three days; remove the chickpea seed coat. Chickpea splits into two cotyledons (dicot); maize has one thin cotyledon (monocot).

2.9 Activity 2.9 — Let us find out (movement)

Make a poster of animals (Fig. 2.7) and list in Table 2.5 how each moves and which body parts it uses.

2.10 Activity 2.10 — Let us compare and analyse (regions)

Recreate Table 2.6 on the blackboard: desert — camel; mountains — deodar tree; ocean — fish; forest — lion. Add more. The plants and animals of one region differ from those of another.

Quick Summary

  • We are surrounded by a large variety of plants and animals — part of biodiversity.
  • Plants and animals can be grouped by similarities and differences; grouping = arranging things by common features.
  • Plants: herbs, shrubs and trees by height, stem type and branching pattern.
  • Plants: dicots and monocots by the number of cotyledons in the seed.
  • Monocots: parallel venation + fibrous roots. Dicots: reticulate venation + taproots.
  • Animals can be grouped by their type of movement.
  • Biodiversity varies between regions because of distinct environmental conditions.
  • Adaptations are special features that let living things survive in a region; a habitat is where they live — terrestrial or aquatic.
  • Habitat damage causes loss of biodiversity; we must protect it so our planet stays full of life.
