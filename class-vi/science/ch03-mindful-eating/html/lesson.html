Core Concepts

1 3.1 What do we eat? — food diversity and culinary practices

People across India eat a great variety of food. What a region eats depends on the crops grown locally, taste preferences, and culture and traditions.

Cooking practices are called culinary practices. They have changed over time:

TraditionalModern
Chulha (earthen stove)Gas stove / electric stove
Sil-batta (stone grinder)Electric grinder / mixer

Reasons: technological development, better transport and better communication.

2 3.2 Components of food — carbohydrates and fats

Carbohydrates are the primary source of energy. Glucose gives instant energy; sugar is also a carbohydrate. Sources: cereals (wheat, rice, maize), potato, sweet potato, banana, pineapple, mango.

Fats are stored energy and give more energy than carbohydrates. Plant sources: groundnuts, walnuts, almonds, pumpkin and sunflower seeds, coconut, oils. Animal sources: curd, butter, ghee.

Fun factPolar bears build a thick layer of fat under their skin as an energy reserve for hibernation.
3 Proteins — body-building foods

Proteins are needed for growth and repair of the body. Sportspersons need more protein to build and repair muscles.

  • Plant sources: pulses (moong, tuar dal), kidney beans, gram, soya bean, peas, nuts; mushrooms too.
  • Animal sources: milk, paneer, egg, fish, meat, chicken.
4 Vitamins and minerals — protective foods

Needed in small amounts, they protect the body from diseases.

  • Vitamin D is made by the body when skin is exposed to sunlight.
  • Some vitamins (especially Vitamin C) are lost with high heat in cooking.
  • Wash vegetables before cutting or peeling — washing after cutting washes vitamins away.
5 Roughage and water

Roughage (dietary fibre) gives no nutrients but helps the body get rid of undigested food and ensures smooth passage of stools (prevents constipation). Sources: green leafy vegetables, fresh fruits, whole grains, pulses, nuts — mainly plant products.

Water helps the body absorb nutrients and removes wastes through sweat and urine.

6 3.3 Testing food for starch, fat and protein
TestMethodPositive result
Starch (Activity 3.5)Add 2–3 drops of dilute iodine solutionBlue-black colour
Fat (Activity 3.6)Wrap and press food in paper; let it dry; hold against lightOily patch that light shines through
Protein (Activity 3.7 — teacher demo)Food paste + water + 2 drops copper sulphate + 10 drops caustic soda; shake, leaveViolet colour

Most foods contain more than one nutrient — e.g. peanuts contain both fat and protein.

PrecautionsCopper sulphate and caustic soda are harmful. Handle with care; wash with plenty of water if spilled on skin or clothes; never taste or smell them.
7 Case studies — scurvy and goitre

Scurvy (1746): Scottish physician James Lind found that sailors on long voyages with bleeding, swollen gums recovered when they ate lemons and oranges — scurvy is caused by Vitamin C deficiency.

Goitre (1960s): Indian scientists found neck swelling common in the Himalayan region and northern plains, where soil (and so crops) lacked iodine. The Government added iodine to common salt — iodised salt.

The Agariyas of the Little Rann of Kutch, Gujarat, traditionally extract salt from seawater, living in desert heat for about 8 months a year.

8 3.4 Balanced diet

Nutritional needs differ with age, gender, physical activity, health status and lifestyle.

A balanced diet has all the essential nutrients, roughage and water in the right amounts for proper growth and development. It gives enough energy-giving, body-building and protective foods.

Lack of one or more nutrients over a long time leads to deficiency diseases.

9 3.5 Millets, packaged food and junk food

Millets — jowar, bajra, ragi, sanwa — are native Indian small-grained cereals. They grow easily in different climatic conditions, have been eaten for centuries, and are rich in vitamins, minerals (iron, calcium) and dietary fibre — hence nutri-cereals.

Packaged food must show nutritional information per serving or per 100 g. Fortification adds nutrients during processing (e.g. iodised salt). FSSAI ensures food quality and safety.

Junk food (wafers, candy bars, fizzy drinks) is high in sugar and fat, low in protein, vitamins, minerals and fibre; eating it often can lead to obesity.

10 3.6 Food miles — from farm to plate

Food miles = the total distance food travels from the farmer to the consumer. Reducing them cuts transport cost and pollution, supports local farmers and keeps food fresher.

Farmer grows wheat → Threshing & winnowing → Storage of grains
  → Grinding & packing → Transport to shop → Food on our plate

Many people waste food. Remember the effort of farmers — take only as much as you can eat. Eating local, plant-based food is good for our bodies and our planet.

"Eat healthy, share, and respect food. Support local producers!"
11 Know a scientist — Coluthur Gopalan (1918–2019)

Pioneer of nutrition research in India. He analysed more than 500 Indian foods, recommended a diet suited to India, and led surveys that found widespread protein and energy deficiencies. His work led to the Mid Day Meal Programme (2002), now PM POSHAN, which gives balanced meals in government and government-aided schools.

Important Rules & Facts

  • Energy-giving: carbohydrates, fats. Body-building: proteins. Protective: vitamins, minerals.
  • Starch + iodine → blue-black. Fat → oily patch on paper. Protein + CuSO₄ + caustic soda → violet.
  • Vit A → night blindness · B₁ → beriberi · C → scurvy · D → rickets · Iodine → goitre · Iron → anaemia.
  • Vitamin D is made in the skin in sunlight; it helps absorb calcium.
  • Wash before cutting vegetables to save vitamins.
  • Balanced diet = all nutrients + roughage + water, in the right amounts.

Press each food in paper, let dry, hold to light. Oily patch = fat.

3.7 Activity 3.7 — test for protein (teacher demo)

Food paste + water + copper sulphate + caustic soda. Violet = protein. Record all results in Table 3.3.

3.8 Activity 3.8 — check your diet

Look at your Activity 3.1 food list: are all nutrients, roughage and water present? Which are missing?

3.9 Activity 3.9 — compare labels

Compare the nutrition labels of potato wafers and roasted chana (per 100 g) — roasted chana has less fat, more protein and about 3.5× the fibre.

Quick Summary

  • People across India eat diverse food; choices depend on local crops, taste, culture and traditions.
  • Culinary practices have changed from traditional (chulha, sil-batta) to modern methods.
  • Food gives energy, supports growth, repairs the body and protects us from disease.
  • Major nutrients: carbohydrates, fats, proteins, vitamins and minerals; food also contains dietary fibre and water.
  • Carbohydrates and fats give energy; proteins build the body; vitamins and minerals protect us.
  • A balanced diet gives all nutrients in the right amounts, with roughage and water.
  • Long-term lack of a nutrient causes deficiency diseases.
  • Junk food is high in sugar and fat but low in protein, vitamins, minerals and fibre.
  • Millets are nutri-cereals and grow easily in different climates.
  • Minimise food miles; eat local, plant-based food; never waste food.
