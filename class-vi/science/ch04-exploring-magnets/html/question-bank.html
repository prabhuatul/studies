A · Fill in the blanks 10 marks

  1. Naturally occurring magnets are called ______________.
  2. Iron, nickel and ______________ are magnetic metals.
  3. Materials not attracted by a magnet are called ______________ materials.
  4. Most iron filings stick near the ______________ of a bar magnet.

  7. The end of a compass needle pointing north is usually painted ______________.
  8. Like poles ______________ each other.

  10. The ancient Indian navigation device made of a fish-shaped iron piece floating in oil was called the ______________.
Answers

  2. cobalt
  3. non-magnetic
  4. poles (ends)
  5. north–south
  6. magnet
  7. red
  8. repel
  9. repulsion
  10. matsya-yantra (machchh-yantra)

B · True or false 10 marks

  1. All metals are attracted by a magnet.
  2. Lodestones are artificial magnets made in factories.
  3. If a bar magnet is broken into two, one piece will have only a North pole.
  4. An iron bar suspended freely always rests in the north–south direction.
  5. The North pole of a magnet will attract the South pole of another magnet.
  6. An iron bar is attracted by both the North and South poles of a magnet.
  7. Placing a sheet of glass between a magnet and a compass stops the needle from deflecting.
  8. To magnetise a needle, the magnet should be rubbed back and forth in both directions.
  9. Magnets should be kept away from mobile phones and remote controls.
  10. Two matchbox cars with magnets whose like poles face each other will rush towards each other.
Answers with reasons
  1. False — only some metals — iron, nickel, cobalt and some of their combinations — are magnetic.
  2. False — lodestones are naturally occurring magnets discovered in ancient times.
  3. False — North and South poles always exist in pairs, even in the smallest piece of a magnet.
  4. False — only a magnet does; an iron bar can rest in any direction.
  5. True — unlike poles attract each other.
  6. True — both ends of an iron bar are attracted by both poles — it never shows repulsion.
  7. False — there is no appreciable change; magnetism acts through non-magnetic materials like glass.
  8. False — it should be moved in one direction with the same pole, lifted, and brought back to the same starting end — 30 to 40 times.
  9. True — this is part of the chapter's "keep magnets safe" advice.
  10. False — like poles repel, so the cars run away from each other.

D · Short answers 5 × 2 = 10 marks

D1 How will you show that the attraction of a bar magnet is strongest at its ends?

Spread iron filings on a sheet of paper and place a bar magnet over them, then tap the paper. The maximum iron filings stick near the two ends of the magnet and very few stick in the middle. These ends, where attraction is strongest, are the poles.

D2 Why does a freely suspended magnet always point in the north–south direction?

Because the Earth itself behaves like a giant magnet. A freely suspended magnet lines up with the Earth's magnetism and comes to rest along the north–south direction, with its North-seeking pole pointing north.

D3 Describe a magnetic compass and how it is used.

It is a small circular box with a transparent cover. A needle-shaped magnet balanced on a pin can rotate freely; its north end is usually painted red, and a dial marked with directions lies below. To use it, place it where you want to find directions, wait for the needle to rest north–south, then rotate the box until N and S on the dial line up with the needle.

D4 Why is repulsion, and not attraction, the sure test for a magnet?

A magnet attracts both another magnet (unlike pole) and a plain iron bar, so attraction cannot tell them apart. But only a magnet can be repelled — when like poles face each other. An iron bar is attracted at both ends by both poles and never repels.

D5 What precautions should be taken to keep magnets safe?

Do not heat, drop or hammer magnets, and keep them away from mobile phones and remote controls. Store bar magnets in pairs with unlike poles on the same side, a piece of wood between them, and two pieces of soft iron placed across their ends.

E · Long answers 2 × 5 = 10 marks

E1 Explain, step by step, how you can make your own magnetic compass at home. How would you check that it works?
  1. Collect a cork, an iron sewing needle, a permanent bar magnet, a glass bowl and water.
  2. Place the needle on a wooden table. Put one pole of the magnet at one end of the needle and move it along the length of the needle to the other end. Lift the magnet up.
  3. Bring the same pole back to the same starting end and repeat. Do this 30 to 40 times.
  4. Bring iron filings or steel pins near the needle. If they are attracted, the needle has become a magnet.
  5. Push the needle horizontally through the cork and float the cork in a bowl of water so the needle stays above the water.

Checking it works: when the needle comes to rest, note the direction each end points. Rotate the cork gently and let it stop again; repeat a few times. If the needle always comes to rest pointing the same way (north–south), the compass works. This is very like the ancient Indian matsya-yantra, a magnetised fish-shaped iron piece floating in oil.

E2 Describe Activity 4.5 and Activity 4.6. What do they teach us about the poles of magnets, and how can this be used to tell a magnet from a piece of iron?

Activity 4.5: magnet A is placed on 5–6 round pencils so it can roll. One end of magnet B is brought near one end of A (without touching), then B's other end. A rolls towards B when unlike poles face each other and rolls away when like poles face each other.

Activity 4.6: when the North pole of a bar magnet is brought near the North end of a compass needle, the needle moves away; when the South pole is brought near, the needle moves closer.

What we learn: unlike poles attract, like poles repel — and a compass needle is itself a magnet.

Telling a magnet from iron: when an iron bar replaces one magnet, both its ends are attracted by both poles — it never repels. So if an object is repelled by some pole of a known magnet, it must be a magnet. Repulsion is the sure test.

F · HOTS 2 × 3 = 6 marks

Higher-order thinking. Reason it out rather than recalling a fact.

F1 A compass needle's North pole points towards the Earth's geographic north. Using the rule of attraction, what kind of magnetic pole must lie near the Earth's geographic north? Explain.

Unlike poles attract. The compass needle's North pole is pulled towards the geographic north, so the Earth's magnetic pole there must be a South magnetic pole. In other words, the Earth's magnet has its South pole near the geographic north and its North pole near the geographic south. (This answers textbook question 8.)

F2 Rohan says, "My magnet picked up a steel pin, so the pin must be a magnet too." Is he right? Plan a simple test to check.

Not necessarily. A magnet attracts any magnetic material — a plain steel pin is attracted even if it is not a magnet. Attraction alone proves nothing.

Test: bring the pin near the North pole of the magnet, then near the South pole, at each end of the pin. If any end is repelled, the pin is a magnet. If both ends are attracted by both poles, it is just magnetic material. (He could also hang the pin freely and see whether it always settles north–south.)

G · Textbook exercises — worked answers

The chapter's own "Let us enhance our learning" questions. Try each one first, then open it.

G1 Q1. Fill in the blanks.

(i) Unlike poles of two magnets attract each other, whereas like poles repel each other. (ii) magnetic materials. (iii) north–south. (iv) two.

G2 Q2. True or False.

(i) F — poles always exist in pairs. (ii) T — like poles repel. (iii) F — filings stick mostly near the ends (poles). (iv) T.

G3 Q3. Column I / Column II — fill in the blanks.

N – N → Repulsion · N – S → Attraction · S – N → Attraction · S – S → Repulsion.

G4 Q4. Atharv's bar magnet and steel U-clips — which observation?

Option (i) 10, 2, 10. Positions A and C are the ends (poles), where attraction is strongest, so many clips stick; B is the middle, where very few stick.

G5 Q5. Three identical bars — two magnets, one iron. How to find the magnets?

Bring the bars near each other in pairs, trying both ends. The two bars that repel each other in some position are the magnets (like poles facing). The iron bar is always attracted, never repelled, by the other bars — so it is the bar that never shows repulsion.

G6 Q6. Find the poles of an unmarked magnet using a marked magnet.

Bring the North pole of the marked magnet near one end of the unmarked magnet. If that end is repelled, it is a North pole (like poles repel); if it is attracted, it is a South pole. The other end is the opposite pole.

G7 Q7. Find the North pole without using another magnet.

Suspend the magnet freely with a thread tied at its middle so it balances horizontally. When it comes to rest, it lies north–south. The end pointing towards the north (find north from where the Sun rises in the east) is the North pole.

G8 Q8. Guess the poles of Earth's magnet from the compass.

The compass needle's North pole points to geographic north. Since unlike poles attract, the Earth's magnetic South pole must be near the geographic north, and its magnetic North pole near the geographic south.

G9 Q9. Screws keep falling off the mechanic's screwdriver.

Magnetise the tip of the steel screwdriver by stroking it with one pole of a magnet many times in the same direction (as in Activity 4.4), or use a screwdriver with a magnet at its tip. The steel screws will then be attracted and stay on the screwdriver.

G10 Q10. Ring magnet X won't move down onto Y.

The faces of X and Y that point towards each other are like poles, so they repel and X stays floating above Y. To bring them together, take X off, turn it upside down and put it back on — now unlike poles face each other, so X is attracted down onto Y.

G11 Q11. Polarity at ends 1, 2, 3, 4 and 6 (end 5 is N).

1 – N, 2 – S, 3 – N, 4 – S, 6 – S (5 – N given).

The three magnets form a Z shape: ends 1–2 (top magnet), 3–4 (upright magnet), 5–6 (bottom magnet), with end 2 touching 3 and end 4 touching 5.

Reasoning: touching ends stay together, so they must be unlike poles. 5 = N → 4 = S → the other end of that magnet, 3 = N → 2 = S → 1 = N. And 6, the other end of the magnet with 5, = S.
