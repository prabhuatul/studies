What this chapter is about

Reshma, a girl from a coastal town in Kerala, is writing a story about sailors lost in a storm with no stars to guide them — and discovers the magnetic compass. Following her curiosity, the chapter explores magnetic and non-magnetic materials (iron, nickel, cobalt), the two poles of every magnet (which can never be separated), how a freely suspended magnet always points north–south because the Earth itself behaves like a giant magnet, how to make your own magnet and compass, the ancient Indian matsya-yantra, the rule that like poles repel and unlike poles attract, and how magnetism acts even through non-magnetic materials. It ends with fun magnet games and how to keep magnets safe.

The six things most worth remembering

1 · Magnetic materials

Materials attracted by a magnet — iron, nickel, cobalt (and some of their combinations). Wood, plastic, rubber, glass are non-magnetic.

2 · Poles come in pairs

Iron filings cling most at the ends (poles). Break a magnet and every piece still has a North and a South pole — a single pole cannot exist.

3 · Magnets point north–south

A freely suspended magnet always rests along the north–south direction, because the Earth behaves like a giant magnet. This is how a compass works.

4 · Like repel, unlike attract

N–N or S–S repel; N–S attract. Repulsion is the sure test for a magnet — an iron bar is attracted by both poles but never repels.

5 · Magnetism passes through

Wood, cardboard, plastic and glass placed between a magnet and a compass do not stop the effect — magnetism acts through non-magnetic materials.

6 · Matsya-yantra

An early Indian navigation device: a magnetised fish-shaped iron piece floating in a vessel of oil.
