Exploring Magnets

Read top to bottom — it follows the order of the chapter. Tick each section off as you finish it.

1 · Natural vs artificial magnets

  • Lodestone = natural magnet, discovered in ancient times; used by olden-day sailors.
  • Artificial magnets — lab magnets, pencil boxes, stickers, toys; first made from iron pieces.
  • Shapes: bar, U-shaped, ring, disc, cylindrical, spherical — every shape has two poles.

2 · Magnetic or not?

MagneticNon-magnetic
Iron, nickel, cobalt (and some combinations) — steel pins, U-clips, iron nailsWood, rubber, plastic, glass, paper, cardboard

3 · Poles

  • Iron filings cling most at the ends → poles; very few in the middle.
  • Two poles: North and South. Break a magnet → every piece still has both.
  • Pole markings: N/S letters, a white dot on North, or red = North, blue = South.

4 · Directions and the compass

  • Freely suspended magnet → rests north–south. Reason: Earth behaves like a giant magnet.
  • An iron bar rests in any direction → suspension test tells a magnet from iron.
  • Compass: needle magnet on a pin, rotates freely, red end points North, dial below, transparent cover.
  • Use: let needle settle → rotate the box until dial N–S matches the needle.
  • Home-made compass: stroke needle 30–40 times, same pole, same direction → cork → bowl of water.
  • Matsya-yantra: magnetised fish-shaped iron in a vessel of oil.

5 · Attraction and repulsion

The most-asked rule in the chapterLike repel, unlike attract.
Poles brought togetherResult
N – NRepulsion

Either pole – iron barAttraction (always)
The sure testRepulsion proves something is a magnet; attraction alone does not.

6 · Through materials, and safety

  • Wood, cardboard, plastic, glass in between → no appreciable change: magnetism acts through non-magnetic materials.
  • Safety: don't heat, drop or hammer; keep away from mobile phones and remote controls.
  • Storage: pairs, unlike poles on the same side, wood between, soft iron pieces across the ends.

7 · 10 facts most likely to be asked

  1. Magnetic materials: iron, nickel, cobalt.
  2. A magnet always has two poles — they exist in pairs.
  3. Iron filings stick most at the poles, least in the middle.
  4. A freely suspended magnet rests north–south.
  5. Earth behaves like a giant magnet.
  6. Like poles repel; unlike poles attract.
  7. Repulsion is the sure test for a magnet.
  8. Magnetism acts through non-magnetic materials.
  9. Lodestone = natural magnet; matsya-yantra = Indian fish-shaped compass in oil.
  10. Store magnets in pairs, unlike poles on the same side, with soft iron across the ends.

Common mistakes to avoid

Don't lose marks here
  • Saying "all metals are magnetic" — only iron, nickel, cobalt (and some combinations) are.
  • Saying a broken magnet gives a "North-only piece" — every piece has both poles.
  • Saying the middle of a bar magnet is strongest — it is the weakest; the poles are strongest.
  • Using attraction to prove an object is a magnet — iron is attracted too; only repulsion proves it.
  • Thinking glass, wood or plastic "block" magnetism — the effect passes through them.
  • When magnetising a needle, rubbing back and forth — you must stroke in one direction with the same pole, lifting the magnet each time.
