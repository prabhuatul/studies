# Science — Chapter 4: Exploring Magnets

> **Class VI | CBSE / NCERT — Curiosity: Textbook of Science, Grade 6**
> Textbook pages 61–78
> Last updated: 2026-10-10

---

## Overview

Reshma, a girl from a coastal town in Kerala, is writing a story about sailors lost in a storm with no stars to guide them — and discovers the **magnetic compass**. Following her curiosity, the chapter explores **magnetic and non-magnetic materials** (iron, nickel, cobalt), the **two poles** of every magnet (which can never be separated), how a **freely suspended magnet** always points **north–south** because the Earth itself behaves like a giant magnet, how to **make your own magnet and compass**, the ancient Indian **matsya-yantra**, the rule that **like poles repel and unlike poles attract**, and how magnetism acts even **through non-magnetic materials**. It ends with fun magnet games and how to keep magnets safe.

---

## Opening Thought

> **Reshma's problem:** In her story, sailors carrying spices from Kerala are caught in a storm under an overcast sky — no stars to guide them. How will they find directions? Her research leads her to the **magnetic compass**.

## Key Terms & Definitions

| Term | Definition |
|---|---|
| Magnet | An object that attracts materials such as iron, nickel and cobalt, and always has a North and a South pole. |
| Lodestone | A naturally occurring magnet, discovered in ancient times; used by sailors in the olden days. |
| Artificial magnet | A magnet made by people — e.g. those in laboratories, pencil boxes, stickers and toys. |
| Magnetic materials | Materials that are attracted towards a magnet — iron, nickel, cobalt and some of their combinations with other metals. |
| Non-magnetic materials | Materials that are not attracted towards a magnet — e.g. wood, rubber, plastic, glass. |
| Iron filings | Very small pieces of iron, used to show where a magnet's attraction is strongest. |
| Poles of a magnet | The two ends of a magnet where most iron filings stick — the North pole and the South pole. |
| North (North-seeking) pole | The end of a freely suspended magnet that points towards the north. |
| South (South-seeking) pole | The end of a freely suspended magnet that points towards the south. |
| Magnetic compass | A small device with a freely rotating magnetic needle that rests in the north–south direction; used to find directions. |
| Attraction | Pulling together — unlike poles (N–S) attract each other. |
| Repulsion | Pushing apart — like poles (N–N or S–S) repel each other. |
| Matsya-yantra (machchh-yantra) | An early Indian navigation device — a magnetised fish-shaped iron piece kept in a vessel of oil. |
| Bar / U-shaped / Ring magnet | Common shapes of magnets (also disc, cylindrical, spherical). Every shape has two poles. |

## Core Concepts

### Reshma's story — why we need magnets

Reshma lives in a **coastal town of Kerala** and loves writing stories. She was writing a story for her grandmother's **60th birthday** about a ship carrying **spices from Kerala** for trade in the olden days. Sailors used **stars** to find directions at night — but in her story a storm brought an overcast sky and the stars were hidden. Searching the internet and her school library, she learnt that travellers used a **magnetic compass** to find directions.

She had seen magnets in **pencil boxes and purses** (to keep them closed) and in a **whiteboard duster**, but had never looked at them carefully. She became curious about magnets.

### Natural and artificial magnets; shapes of magnets

- Sailors in the olden days used **lodestones** — **naturally occurring magnets** discovered in ancient times.

- Later, people found that magnets could be made from **pieces of iron**. Today magnets are made of different materials.

- Magnets in the school laboratory, pencil boxes, stickers and toys are **artificial magnets**.

- Shapes (Fig. 4.2 and p.78): **bar, U-shaped, ring, disc, cylindrical, spherical**. "My poles always occur in pairs, no matter my shape."

### 4.1 Magnetic and non-magnetic materials

Activity 4.1: predict, then test, which objects stick to a magnet.

- **Magnetic materials** are attracted towards a magnet. **Iron** is magnetic; **nickel and cobalt** are other magnetic metals; some of their combinations with other metals are also attracted.

- **Non-magnetic materials** are not attracted — e.g. a wooden pencil, a rubber eraser, plastic, glass.

> **Careful:** Not every metal is magnetic. The chapter names only iron, nickel and cobalt (and some combinations).

### 4.2 Poles of a magnet

Activity 4.2: spread iron filings on paper and place a bar magnet over them. **Maximum filings stick near the ends**; very few stick in the middle.

- These ends are the **two poles** — the **North pole** and the **South pole**.

- Most filings stick to the poles of a magnet of **any shape**.

- **A magnet with a single pole is not possible.** If a magnet is broken into pieces, North and South poles exist in pairs even in the **smallest piece**.

### 4.3 Finding directions — the freely suspended magnet

Activity 4.3: hang a bar magnet from a thread tied at its middle so it balances horizontally; rotate it gently and let it rest. Mark the line. Push it again — it rests along the **same line**.

- **A freely suspended magnet comes to rest along the north–south direction.**

- The end pointing north = **North-seeking (North) pole**; the other = **South-seeking (South) pole**.

- It does this because **our Earth itself behaves like a giant magnet**.

- An **iron bar** suspended the same way can rest in **any** direction — so this activity is a way to **test whether a piece of metal is a magnet**.

- Hint for finding which way is north: the Sun rises roughly in the **East** and sets in the **West**.

### The magnetic compass

- A small **circular box with a transparent cover**.

- A **needle-shaped magnet** is balanced on a **pin** so it can **rotate freely**.

- The end of the needle pointing **North** is usually painted **red**. Below the needle is a **dial** marked with directions.

- **How to use it:** keep it where you want to know directions; wait for the needle to rest north–south; gently rotate the box until N and S on the dial line up with the needle. Now all directions are as shown on the dial.

### Make your own magnet and compass (Activity 4.4)

- Place an **iron sewing needle** on a wooden table.

- Put **one pole** of a bar magnet at one end of the needle and move it **along the length** to the other end; **lift it up**.

- Bring the **same pole** back to the **same starting end** and repeat — **30 to 40 times**.

- Test: if iron filings or steel pins are attracted, the needle has become a magnet.

- Push the needle horizontally through a **cork** and float it in a glass bowl of **water**, needle above the water. When it rests, your compass is ready — it always points the same way.

> **Do you know? — Matsya-yantra:** Long before the modern compass, Indians navigated at sea using the **matsya-yantra** (or **machchh-yantra**): a **magnetised fish-shaped iron piece kept in a vessel of oil** — very like the floating-needle compass you just made.

### 4.4 Attraction and repulsion between magnets

Activity 4.5: rest magnet A on 5–6 round pencils; bring each end of magnet B near one end of A without touching.

- **Unlike poles attract** (North of one + South of the other).

- **Like poles repel** (North + North, or South + South).

- With an **iron bar** instead of one magnet: **both ends** of the bar are attracted by **both poles** of the magnet — no repulsion.

> **Key conclusion:** **A magnet can be identified by its property of repulsion.** Attraction alone doesn't prove something is a magnet — an iron bar is attracted too.

Activity 4.6: a compass needle is also a magnet. Bring the **North pole** of a bar magnet near the North end of the needle → the needle **moves away**. Bring the **South pole** near it → the needle **moves closer**.

### Magnetism acts through non-magnetic materials (Activity 4.7)

Place a piece of **wood** between the bar magnet and the compass, then try **cardboard, thin plastic and thin glass**. There is **no appreciable change** in the needle's deflection.

**Conclusion: the magnetic effect can act through non-magnetic materials.** "Magnets can move some objects without touching them!"

### 4.5 Fun with magnets

- **Magnetic garland** — pins/clips hanging in a chain from a magnet.

- **Maze game** — guide steel balls out of a cardboard maze by moving a magnet **below** the tray (works through cardboard).

- **Paper clip in water** — pull a steel clip out of a glass of water with a magnet outside, without wetting fingers or magnet.

- **Matchbox-magnet cars** — with **like poles** facing, the cars **run away** from each other.

**How poles are marked:** N and S letters; or a **white dot** for North; or North painted **red** and South **blue**.

### Keeping magnets safe

- **Do not heat, drop or hammer** magnets.

- Do not keep them near **mobile phones or remote controls**.

- **Store in pairs** with **unlike poles on the same side**, a **piece of wood** between them, and **two pieces of soft iron** across the ends.

## Important Rules / Formulas

- Magnetic: **iron, nickel, cobalt** (and some combinations). Non-magnetic: wood, rubber, plastic, glass.
- Every magnet has **two poles**; they **always exist in pairs**.
- Attraction is strongest at the **poles**, weakest in the middle.
- A freely suspended magnet rests **north–south** (Earth behaves like a giant magnet).
- **Like poles repel; unlike poles attract.**
- **Repulsion** is the sure test for a magnet.
- Magnetic effect acts **through non-magnetic materials**.
- Magnetise a needle by stroking it 30–40 times with the **same pole in the same direction**.

## Activities

### Activity 4.1 — Let us explore (which materials stick?)

Collect objects of different materials; predict which will stick to a magnet (Table 4.1), then test. Pencil (wood) and eraser (rubber) do not; iron objects do.

### Activity 4.2 — Let us investigate (iron filings)

Bar magnet over iron filings; tap the paper. Filings cluster at the ends → the poles.

### Activity 4.3 — Let us experiment (suspended magnet)

Suspend a bar magnet by a thread at its middle; it always comes to rest along the same line — north–south. Repeat with an iron bar: it rests in any direction.

### Activity 4.4 — Let us construct (your own compass)

Magnetise a sewing needle by stroking it with one pole 30–40 times, push it through a cork, float it in water — a home-made compass.

### Activity 4.5 — Let us experiment (two magnets)

Magnet A on round pencils; bring each end of magnet B near it. Unlike poles attract, like poles repel. With an iron bar, both ends are always attracted.

### Activity 4.6 — Let us experiment (magnet and compass)

N pole of bar magnet near N of compass needle → needle moves away; S pole → needle moves closer.

### Activity 4.7 — Let us investigate (through materials)

Place wood, cardboard, plastic and glass sheets between magnet and compass (Table 4.2) — no appreciable change in deflection.

## Quick Summary

- A magnet has two poles — the **North pole** and the **South pole**.
- Poles **always exist in pairs**; a single North or South pole cannot exist.
- **Magnetic materials** are attracted towards a magnet; **non-magnetic materials** are not.
- A **freely suspended magnet** rests along the **north–south** direction.
- The needle of a **magnetic compass** indicates the north–south direction.
- Like poles (N–N, S–S) **repel**; unlike poles (N–S) **attract**.
- Magnetic effect can act **through non-magnetic materials**.
- Treat magnets with care — do not heat, drop or hammer them; store them in pairs.
